01Start here

The manual holds everything. Your week runs on very little of it. This page shows which parts to use and when.

The first two weeks

  1. Day 1, 45 minutes. Do the Baseline tests you can do today and score yourself in the Scorecard. Save the snapshot.
  2. Day 2 to 7. Run the time audit and the 7-night sleep log from Baseline tests. Start Body, Stage 1, Week 1. It needs only the kit you already own.
  3. End of week 1, 60 minutes. Fill in Direction. This decides what the next ten years are for.
  4. Week 2. Set up your first 12-week sprint on the Sprint board, with Body as the focus. Put the weekly review in your calendar.
  5. Every quarter. Retest, rescore, archive the sprint, choose the next focus.

Three depths

  • Summary. Each domain opens with a one-page summary. That is enough to run a quarter.
  • Protocol. The staged plan underneath tells you what to do each week.
  • Playbooks. The collapsed rows marked with a plus are step-by-step guides for specific situations. Open one when you need it. "Open all playbooks" at the top of the page expands every one.

Two rules that protect the system

One focus per quarter. Every other domain runs at its Floor. Overhauling seven areas at once is the most reliable way to quit by week three.

Reading is not doing. Adding content to this manual, rebuilding it as an app, or studying a domain you are not working on all feel like progress. Only ticked standards and retested numbers count.

Everything you type or tick is saved automatically, and each standard records the date you first met it. Use the backup at the bottom of Rhythm once a year. Body, Mind, Craft, Money and Presence are complete in this version. People and Character, and two new domains, get the same depth in the next release.

02The standard

What "top 1% man" means here, stated so it can be measured.

You said you do not want the watered-down version. Agreed. Here is the version that is both ambitious and mathematically real.

The arithmetic of being rare

Being the best in the world at one thing is mostly unavailable. Being very good across every domain at once is rare and available. If four qualities were unrelated and you reached the top 30% in each, you would be in roughly the top 1% for the combination (0.3 × 0.3 × 0.3 × 0.3 = 0.8%). Top 25% in three is 1.6%.

Real traits are correlated, so the true figure is less extreme. The principle holds. Most people who are strong in one domain have let another collapse: the high earner who is unfit, the athlete who is broke, the achiever who is alone. The top 1% man is the one with no collapsed domain and one or two spikes.

The definition this manual uses

  • No domain below Strong. All seven domains at the Strong tier or better.
  • Two spikes at Elite. For you the natural spikes are Craft and one of Body or Presence.
  • Held for years. A standard you hold for one quarter is a stunt. The target is to still be there at 34.

03Principles

The psychology the system runs on, with an honest rating of how strong the evidence is.

Self-improvement content repeats findings that have not held up. Each principle below carries a rating: solid well replicated or consensus, useful real but smaller than advertised, contested do not build on it.

1. Confidence is a record, not a feeling solid

In Bandura's work on self-efficacy, the strongest source of belief in your ability is having actually done the hard thing before. Watching others and being encouraged help less.17 So you do not work on confidence directly. You stack small completed challenges and the belief follows. Every domain here starts with a Floor you can hit this month for that reason.

2. Pursue goals you endorse solid

Self-determination theory identifies three needs behind lasting motivation: autonomy (you chose it), competence (you are getting better), relatedness (you are connected to people while doing it). Motivation and wellbeing rise when they are met and fall when they are blocked.16 A goal you adopted to impress people fails the first test. The Direction section exists to filter those out.

3. Write if-then plans useful

"If it is 6:30 on Monday, Wednesday or Friday, then I go to the gym before anything else." A 2006 meta-analysis of 94 tests found a medium-to-large effect on goal attainment (d = 0.65).9 A much larger 2024 synthesis that corrected for publication bias put the effect far lower, between 0.15 and 0.35.10 Use it, because it costs nothing. Do not expect it to carry the load.

4. Imagine the obstacle, not just the win useful

WOOP: Wish, Outcome, Obstacle, Plan. Pairing a vivid goal with the specific inner obstacle and an if-then plan shows a small-to-medium effect across 21 studies (g = 0.34), and the authors note the true figure may be smaller.11 Pure positive visualisation is not part of this system.

5. Habits take months, and one miss is harmless solid

The study behind the "66 days" figure followed 96 volunteers for 12 weeks. The median time to automaticity was 66 days, with a range from 18 to 254, and missing a single day made no material difference.8 Only about half the participants fitted the habit curve at all. Plan on two to eight months per habit, and treat the first miss as nothing and the second in a row as an alarm.

6. Design the environment. Do not rely on willpower contested

The idea that willpower is a tank that drains has not survived testing. A preregistered project across 36 labs and 3,531 people found an effect near zero (d = 0.06), with the data four times more likely under no effect.12 Stop asking whether you have enough discipline today. Remove the decision: gym bag packed the night before, phone charging outside the bedroom, investments debited on salary day.

7. In knowledge work, hours are not the edge solid

Deliberate practice explains about a quarter of performance differences in games, a fifth in music and under 1% in professions.13 Practice still matters. In a career, the differentiators are which problems you work on, who gives you feedback and which opportunities you are near. The Craft section is built around that.

8. Learn by testing yourself, spaced out solid

A review of ten study techniques rated two as high utility: practice testing and distributed practice. Rereading and highlighting, the most common methods, rated low.14 For anything you want to keep: close the book, write what you remember, repeat after days and then weeks.

9. Skip the mindset and grit products contested

Growth-mindset programmes show an average effect on achievement close to zero (d = 0.05 across 63 studies), though a competing analysis finds real benefits for at-risk students.15 Grit turns out to be nearly the same thing as conscientiousness and only moderately related to performance.18 You do not need a special belief. You need the plain version of conscientiousness: do what you said, on schedule, checked.

10. Three thinking tools worth owning

  • The control split (Stoic). Sort every worry into "mine to act on" and "not mine". Act on the first list. Drop the second.
  • The thought record (CBT). Situation, automatic thought, evidence for, evidence against, balanced thought. Five lines on paper when your mind is running a story.
  • Values over moods (ACT). Ask "what would the person I am building do in the next ten minutes?" and do that with the mood still present.

Not yet sourced

These are widely repeated. The verdicts below are judgement and have not yet been tied to a specific study in this manual. They are on the list for the next research sweep.

  • Dopamine detox. The name misdescribes how dopamine works. What helps is the ordinary part: fewer compulsive inputs.
  • Waking at 5 a.m. A regular wake time matters. The hour does not. Sleeping less to wake earlier is a net loss.
  • Cold showers, semen retention and "testosterone-boosting" supplements. No convincing evidence that any of these changes hormones or performance in healthy young men. Sleep, training, body fat and alcohol are what move the needle.
  • Alpha and beta males. Borrowed from early wolf research that its own author later corrected. It is a poor model of human status, which rests on competence and generosity as much as dominance.
  • "93% of communication is body language." A misreading of two small 1960s experiments about conflicting signals. Words carry most of the content in almost every real exchange.
  • Multitasking. Switching between tasks costs time and accuracy on both. Single-task blocks are a core part of Mind.

The filter for anything new

Before adopting any technique, ask three questions. Was it tested on more than a few dozen people? Did anyone outside the original team repeat it? Would it change what I do this week? If you cannot answer yes to all three, park it in the research inbox in the repository.

05How change works

The mechanics of changing behaviour over years, so that the dip in week four does not surprise you.

Lead measures and lag measures

A lag measure is the result you want: waist size, a promotion, net worth. It moves slowly and you cannot act on it directly. A lead measure is the action that produces it: training sessions done, deep-work hours, rupees invested. You can hit a lead measure this week.

Every domain in this manual lists both. Plan and review on lead measures. Check lag measures monthly or quarterly. Judging yourself weekly on a lag measure is the fastest way to lose heart, because it barely moves in a week.

The shape of a new habit

PeriodWhat it feels likeWhat to do
Week 1 to 2Novelty carries you. It feels easy and you want to add more.Do not add. Do less than you could and never miss.
Week 3 to 7Novelty is gone and the habit is not yet automatic. This is where most plans end.Shrink the action if needed. Protect the cue and the time slot above everything.
Week 8 to 12It starts to happen without debate on most days.Now raise the difficulty, a little.

The timings follow the habit research: early repetitions add the most automaticity, the median plateau is around day 66, and complex habits such as exercise take longer.8 The week labels are a guide, not a finding.

Why plans fail

  • Too many changes at once. Each new behaviour competes for the same attention.
  • Outcome goals with no action attached. "Get fit" has no Tuesday.
  • No fixed cue. "When I have time" never arrives.
  • All or nothing. One miss is read as failure and the whole plan is dropped.
  • Unchanged surroundings. The same room, phone and schedule produce the same behaviour.
  • Borrowed goals. A goal you do not own gets abandoned the first time it costs something.

The four moves this manual uses

  1. One focus. A single domain per 12-week sprint, with two or three lead measures.
  2. Fixed cue. Every action has a day, a time and a place, written as an if-then plan.
  3. A floor. A version so small it survives your worst day: the minimum viable day in Rhythm.
  4. Evidence of progress. Ticked standards with dates, retested baselines, quarterly snapshots. Belief in yourself is built from that record.17

11Assets and constraints

A plan that ignores your real starting position is a wish. State both sides plainly, once, and update them yearly.

What you start with

  • An uncommon combination. An engineering degree, an MBA in HR and the ability to build software and AI automation. Few people hold all three.
  • A job in hand. You begin your career with an offer accepted, which removes the pressure most of your cohort is under and frees the coming months for foundation work.
  • Time. At 24, ten years of compounding still leaves you at 34.
  • Proof that you ship. You already build and finish projects. That habit transfers.

How to think about constraints

A constraint is a fact that narrows your options: a credential, a location, money, an obligation, your health. It is different from an excuse, which is a constraint you have not tested. For each one, write the workaround. Most early-career constraints stop mattering once you have two or three results with numbers attached.

The lifting and running numbers are common coaching benchmarks for a healthy man in his twenties. They are judgement, not research findings. Adjust for your frame and any injuries. Your starting numbers go in Baseline tests.

What the evidence says

  • Weekly dose. WHO guidance for adults is 150 to 300 minutes of moderate aerobic activity or 75 to 150 vigorous, plus muscle strengthening for all major groups on 2 or more days.2
  • Light weights work if the sets are hard. A meta-analysis of 21 studies found similar muscle growth from light and heavy loads when sets were taken to failure. Heavy loads were better for maximal strength.22 Your home kit is enough for the first six to nine months.
  • Volume. Growth rises with weekly sets per muscle. Ten or more beats fewer than five, and 12 to 20 hard sets a week is a reasonable range once trained.4
  • Cardio fitness and lifespan. In 122,007 adults given treadmill tests, the fittest group had about a fifth of the death risk of the least fit, with no upper limit to the benefit. Observational, in clinic patients.3
  • Push-ups as a marker. Among 1,104 male firefighters followed for 10 years, those who could do more than 40 push-ups had far fewer cardiovascular events than those who managed fewer than 10. The authors caution it may not generalise beyond active middle-aged men, and it marks fitness rather than causing it.25
  • Protein. Across 49 trials, extra protein improved strength and muscle gain from lifting, with no further benefit above roughly 1.6 g per kg per day, and source did not matter.1
  • Sleep amount. The consensus is 7 or more hours a night on a regular basis.5
  • Sleep regularity. In 60,977 adults wearing activity trackers, more regular sleep and wake times went with 20 to 48% lower risk of death, and regularity predicted mortality better than duration did. Observational, in older UK adults.23
  • Caffeine. In a small lab study, 400 mg taken even 6 hours before bed cut sleep by more than an hour, and participants did not notice.24
  • Alcohol. For men aged 15 to 39, the intake that minimises health risk is about a tenth of a standard drink a day. At your age there is no health case for drinking.26
  • Waist. NICE advises keeping your waist under half your height. A ratio of 0.4 to 0.49 indicates no increased risk.6
  • Mood. A network meta-analysis of 218 trials found walking or jogging, yoga and strength training produced moderate reductions in depression, though the authors rate confidence in the evidence as low.28

How to progress

  • Rest 60 to 90 seconds between sets. Lower every rep under control.
  • Start at the bottom of the rep range. Add a rep or two each session.
  • When every set reaches the top of the range, make the exercise harder next time: a heavier dumbbell, a 3-second lowering phase, a pause at the bottom, an added band, or a one-leg or one-arm version.

  • Write every session in the training log: exercise, load, reps.

Sleep the method behind the 7-hour standard
  1. Fix the wake time first. Same time all seven days, within an hour. Regularity is the strongest lever in the research and the easiest to control.
  2. Count back 8 hours for lights out. That gives 7 or more asleep.
  3. Get daylight early. Ten minutes outside within an hour of waking.
  4. Stop caffeine 8 hours before bed. Tea, coffee and energy drinks all count.
  5. Protect the last hour. Dim the lights. Phone on charge outside the bedroom, or across the room if you share one. Read on paper.
  6. Set the room. Cool, dark, quiet. In a hostel or shared flat, an eye mask and earplugs solve most of it.
  7. If you are awake after about 20 minutes, get up, read in dim light, and return when sleepy. Lying there trains the bed to mean wakefulness.
  8. Naps under 30 minutes, before 3 p.m.
  9. Late nights happen. Keep the wake time anyway and take a short nap. Shifting the whole schedule costs you three days.

Steps 1 and 4 rest on the sources above. Steps 3, 5, 6 and 8 are standard sleep-hygiene advice and step 7 comes from behavioural treatment for insomnia. If you snore heavily or are tired despite enough sleep, see a doctor.

What the evidence says

  • Learning. Of ten study techniques reviewed, self-testing and spacing rated highest. Rereading and highlighting rated low.14 Matching study to a "learning style" has no adequate evidence behind it.30
  • Meditation. A review of 47 trials found mindfulness programmes gave small to moderate improvements in anxiety, depression and pain, with little or no evidence for attention, sleep or positive mood, and no advantage over other active treatments such as exercise.27 Worth ten minutes a day. Not a superpower.
  • Exercise. Walking or jogging, yoga and strength training show moderate effects on depression across 218 trials, with low confidence in the evidence.28 Your Body sessions are also mental-health sessions.
  • Willpower. The depletion effect is close to zero in large replications.12 Arrange the room. Do not rely on resolve.
  • Confidence. The strongest source of belief in your ability is having done the hard thing before.17 That is the reasoning behind the pressure drill.

The deep-work session a script for 60 to 90 minutes
  1. Before. Write the single task and what "done" looks like on paper. Phone in another room. Close mail and chat. Water on the desk.
  2. First 5 minutes. Start with the smallest concrete step. Resistance peaks at the start and fades once you are moving.
  3. During. When a stray thought or errand appears, write it on the paper and return. Do not act on it.
  4. If stuck for 10 minutes. Write down exactly what you do not understand. That usually unsticks it. If not, switch to the next sub-task, not to your phone.
  5. After. One line in the weekly notes: what you did, and where to start tomorrow. Then a real break away from screens.

Count only minutes spent on the task. A block with two phone checks is not a deep-work block.

Reading and notes how a book becomes usable knowledge

Staying level under pressure four tools, in the order to reach for them
  1. Slow the breath. Breathe out for longer than you breathe in, for two minutes. It is the fastest lever you have on a racing body.
  2. Name it. "This is anxiety about the presentation." Naming the state puts a little distance between you and it.
  3. Split control. Two columns: mine to act on, not mine. Write one action for the first column. Leave the second.
  4. Act on values. Ask what the man you are building would do in the next ten minutes, and do it with the feeling still present. Waiting to feel ready is the trap.

The pressure drill. Calm is trained by exposure, in steps. List ten situations that make your pulse rise, from mild to severe: asking a question in a large meeting, cold-messaging someone senior, disagreeing with a manager, speaking without slides. Do one a week, starting from the mildest. Log it in the Courage log in Character.

These four tools are standard practice in cognitive and acceptance-based therapy. They are listed here as judgement, without specific trial citations.

Stillness practice ten minutes, no app required
  1. Sit upright, same place and time each day. A timer for ten minutes.
  2. Attend to the breath at the nostrils or belly. Count each out-breath from one to ten, then start again.
  3. When you notice you have drifted, and you will within seconds, return to one. That return is the repetition. It is the whole exercise.
  4. Do not judge the session. A distracted ten minutes still counts.

The alternative. Ten minutes of longhand writing: what is on my mind, what am I avoiding, what matters today. Some people get more from this. Pick one and run it for eight weeks before deciding.

Phone and screens a one-time setup that removes most of the fight
  • Remove social and video apps from the home screen. Log out after each use.
  • Turn off every notification except calls and messages from people.
  • Set app limits on the phone for the two apps that take the most time. Have someone else set the passcode.
  • Greyscale display after 9 p.m.
  • A cheap alarm clock, so the phone has no reason to be near the bed.
  • One fixed window a day for feeds and video, after the work is done.
  • Check the weekly screen report in the weekly review. It is your lead measure.

For compulsive use of any kind, including pornography and gambling apps, the same approach applies: remove access and cues first, and talk to a professional if it does not shift. A vices playbook comes with the Character release.

Decisions for anything costly or hard to reverse
  1. State it. The decision, the options including "do nothing", and the deadline.
  2. Base rate. How does this usually turn out for people in my position? Start from that, then adjust.
  3. Pre-mortem. Imagine it is a year later and it failed. Write why. Fix what you can now.
  4. Three horizons. How will I feel about this in ten days, ten months, ten years?
  5. Ask one person who has done it and has no stake in your choice.
  6. Decide, then record. In the decision journal: what I chose, what I expect, how confident I am as a percentage.
  7. Review at six months. Was the reasoning sound, separate from the result? This is how judgement improves.

Reversible decisions deserve minutes. Only irreversible ones deserve this process.

When to get help maintenance, the same as a physio for a knee

See a doctor, psychologist or psychiatrist if any of these last two weeks or more: low or flat mood most days, loss of interest in things you normally enjoy, constant worry you cannot switch off, sleep or appetite badly off, panic attacks, or using alcohol or anything else to get through the day.

In India, Tele-MANAS is a free government mental-health line on 14416, available round the clock in many languages. If you are ever in immediate danger, call 112.

  • Emergency fund. Six months of total monthly spend. Keep it boring and reachable within a day: a separate savings account, sweep-in deposit or liquid fund. It is not an investment and does not need a return.
  • What it is for. Job loss, medical costs not covered, an urgent trip home, a broken laptop you need for work. Not holidays or phones.
  • Health insurance. A personal policy in addition to the employer's group cover, because the group cover ends when the job does. Buying young keeps premiums low and waiting periods behind you. Check your parents' cover too. Their medical bills are the largest risk to your plan.
  • Term life insurance. Buy it when someone depends on your income or you take a large loan. Plain term cover only.
  • Keep insurance and investment separate. Products that mix the two, such as endowment and unit-linked plans, usually do both jobs poorly.

Cover amounts depend on your city, family and health. Ask an adviser who charges a fee and earns no commission.

Tax basics what to understand, without the section numbers
  • India has two tax regimes: one with lower rates and few deductions, one with higher rates and more deductions. Run your own numbers through the government calculator each year and pick the cheaper one.
  • Your employer deducts tax every month based on what you declare. Declare accurately in April and again when asked for proofs.
  • Read your salary slip until you can explain every line: basic, allowances, provident fund, professional tax, tax deducted.
  • File your own return on the government portal at least once. Match it against the annual tax statement and your Form 16.
  • Capital gains on funds and shares are taxed when you sell, at rates that depend on the asset and holding period. Buying and holding defers this.
  • Never choose an investment only for a tax saving. A bad product with a deduction is still a bad product.
Credit and debt use credit, never carry it
  • A credit score runs from 300 to 900. Above 750 is generally treated as good by lenders.
  • It is built by paying on time, using a small part of your limit, and keeping old accounts open.
  • Pay the full statement amount, automatically. The minimum-due option is the most expensive loan you can take.
  • No "buy now, pay later", personal loans or instalment plans for things that lose value.
  • An education loan or a sensible home loan can be reasonable debt. Clear the costliest loan first.
  • Do not lend, or sign as guarantor, an amount you cannot afford to lose. If you lend to family or friends, treat it as a gift in your own accounts.
Big purchases phone, vehicle, home
  • The 30-day rule. For anything over an amount you set, wait 30 days. Most wants do not survive it.
  • Vehicle. A common heuristic: at least 20% down, a loan of four years or less, and total running cost under a tenth of income. Prefer no loan at all in your twenties.
  • Home. Renting is not waste when you may change city. Compare the full cost of owning (interest, maintenance, taxes, the return your down payment would have earned) against rent. Buy when you expect to stay seven years or more, and the instalment fits inside your needs bucket.
  • Status goods. Watches, phones and cars are signals. Buy them from the wants bucket after the future bucket is paid, or not at all.

These are heuristics and judgement. Use the house, vehicle and job frameworks in Decisions for the actual choice.

What the evidence says

  • First impressions are instant. In five experiments, judgements of competence, trustworthiness and likeability made after seeing a face for a tenth of a second closely matched judgements made with no time limit. More time raised confidence, not accuracy.35 People decide fast and then look for confirmation, so the first seconds are worth preparing. The lead author also notes that these impressions are revised as people get to know you.
  • Posture tricks do not work. Power posing failed to change hormones or behaviour in replication.29
  • Composure is trained by doing. Belief in your ability comes mainly from having done the thing before.17 Speaking repetitions are the method. There is no shortcut around them.

Playbooks

Speaking clearly structure, pace and fillers
  • Answer first. Conclusion in the first sentence, then two or three reasons, then stop. "Yes, we should, for three reasons."
  • One idea per sentence. Short sentences are easier to say well and easier to hear.
  • Pause instead of filler. When you feel "um" coming, close your mouth for a beat. Silence reads as control.
  • Slow the first sentence. Nerves speed you up. Deliver the opening line deliberately and the rest follows.
  • Land the end of sentences. Do not trail off or let a statement rise like a question.
  • Numbers and examples. One specific number or story persuades more than three adjectives.

The weekly tape. Two minutes, unscripted, on any topic. Watch once with sound, once without. Note one thing to fix. Record the filler count in Baseline tests each quarter.

Presenting a ten-minute talk that works
  1. One sentence first. Write the single thing the audience should remember. If you cannot, you are not ready.
  2. Structure. Problem, what you propose, evidence, what you need from them. Three points at most.
  3. Open with the point, not with thanks, an agenda or an apology.
  4. Slides support you. Few words, one idea each. Never read them out.
  5. Rehearse aloud three times, standing, with a timer. Once in front of a person.
  6. Prepare the five hardest questions and your answers.
  7. In the room. Arrive early, test the screen, stand still when making a point, look at one person per sentence.
  8. When you do not know, say so and say when you will. It builds more credibility than a guess.
  9. End on the ask. Then stop.
Writing the one-page memo
  • Subject line or title that states the decision needed.
  • First paragraph: the recommendation and the single most important reason.
  • Then the evidence, in order of importance. Numbers with sources.
  • Then risks and what you would do about each.
  • Last line: what you need, from whom, by when.
  • Cut every sentence that would not be missed. Read it aloud before sending.

Clear writing is clear thinking made visible. It is also the fastest way to be noticed by people senior to you who never see you speak.

Listening and conversation where warmth comes from
  • Three questions before one statement. In any new conversation, ask three real questions before offering an opinion.
  • Follow the last answer. Your next question should come from what they just said, not from your list.
  • Summarise back. "So the real problem is the handover, not the tool?" People feel understood when they hear their point returned accurately.
  • Names. Say it back when you hear it. Write it down afterwards with one detail.
  • Do not top their story. Let it be theirs.
  • Leave well. End conversations a little early, with something specific: "I will send you that article."

Body language and room behaviour what is worth doing
  • Stand and sit tall, shoulders relaxed. Take up your space without spreading.
  • Keep your hands visible and still. Gesture when making a point, then rest.
  • Look at people when they speak and when you finish a sentence.
  • Move slowly. Hurry signals low status in any room.
  • Phone face down or away, always.

  • On video: camera at eye level, light in front of you, look at the lens when making your main point.
Etiquette dining, meetings and travel
  • Introductions. Stand, look at the person, say your full name clearly. Introduce the junior person to the senior one.
  • Dining. Wait for the host to start. Order something easy to eat. Match the pace of the table. Treat serving staff well. People watch this more than anything else.
  • Who pays. Whoever invited. Offer once, sincerely, and accept the answer.
  • Mail and messages. Reply within a day, even if only to say when you will answer properly. Check names and spellings before sending.
  • Meetings. Agenda in advance if you called it. End on time. Send the decisions and owners afterwards.
  • Thank-yous. Specific and within 24 hours.
  • Travel. Carry-on packed light, documents ready, early to the gate. Calm travellers are noticed.
Staying composed when challenged the Specter moment
  1. Pause. One breath out before answering. It looks like thought, because it is.
  2. Restate the challenge fairly. "You are saying the numbers do not hold for smaller teams." This buys time and shows you are not threatened.
  3. Concede what is true. "That is right for teams under ten."
  4. Then make your point, once, with the evidence.
  5. Lower your voice as theirs rises.
  6. If it is an insult, not a challenge, name the topic and return to it. "Let us stay with the proposal." Do not trade.

22When it breaks

It will. The plan for failure is part of the system.

You missed a day

Nothing happened. In the habit research a single missed day had no material effect.8 Do the minimum viable day tomorrow. The rule is never two in a row.

You missed two weeks

  1. Do not restart on Monday. Restart within the hour, with the smallest version.
  2. Run only the three non-negotiables for seven days.
  3. In the weekly review, write what changed in your environment. Travel, a new job and a breakup are the usual causes. Fix the environment and leave your character out of it.

You have plateaued

  • Body: check sleep and food before changing the programme.
  • Craft: you have probably outgrown your feedback. Find a harder problem or a tougher reviewer.
  • Everything: a flat quarter after two strong ones is consolidation. Hold the Floor and wait.

You are burning out

Signs: sleep worsening, dreading things you used to like, getting ill often, snapping at people. Drop to Floor in every domain for two weeks, protect sleep, see your inner five. If it persists, see a doctor.

The standard has turned on you

"Best who can ever be" is a direction. Used as a verdict on your worth, it produces the Wayne failure: a capable, joyless man. Two checks each quarter. Did I enjoy anything that had no payoff? Would the people in my inner five say I have been good to be around? If either answer is no, that is the constraint to fix next, ahead of any score.

25Decisions

Five decisions that shape a decade, each with the questions to answer before you commit.

Use the general method in the Decisions playbook under Mind for every one of these: base rate, pre-mortem, three horizons, one outside view, then record it. The frameworks below add the questions specific to each case. They are judgement, built from common practice.

Changing jobs stay, move inside, or leave
QuestionStay ifMove if
Am I still learning fast?You are doing things you could not do six months agoThe last new skill was more than a year ago
Is my manager good for me?They give feedback, scope and creditThey block, ignore or take credit, and a transfer is not possible
Is there a next role here?You can name it and someone senior has agreed the pathThe path does not exist or keeps moving
What is the market paying?You are within range, or the learning offsets the gapThe gap is large and has lasted two cycles
Does the new role move me toward ownership?It is the same job with a new logoIt adds scope, revenue exposure or a team
  • Leave toward something, never only away from something.
  • Ask internally first. A move inside keeps your reputation and often gets the same result.
  • Finish what you started. Leave with a win logged and a manager who would rehire you.
  • Less than twelve months in a first job needs a strong reason.
Starting a company when the side project becomes the job
  • Have the five gates in the Craft founder playbook been passed in order?
  • Do you hold 12 months of personal expenses, separate from company money?
  • Is there a co-founder whose skills cover what yours do not, and have you worked together under pressure?
  • Does anyone depend on your income, and have you agreed the plan with them?
  • What is the date and the metric at which you stop and go back to a job? Write it before you start.
  • What does your employment contract say about what you built while employed?

The base rate for new companies is failure. That is a reason to stage the risk with gates, and it is not a reason to avoid trying once the gates are passed.

Moving city for a role, a person or a life
  • What does the move add to Craft: scope, pay, industry density, people to learn from?
  • What does it cost in rent and travel? Recalculate the savings rate.
  • Who do you leave, and what is the plan to keep each of the inner five?
  • Who do you know there? Line up three people to meet in the first month.
  • Where will you train, and where will you live relative to work? A long commute taxes every other domain.
  • How far is it from your parents, in hours door to door?
A big purchase vehicle, home, anything over a month of pay
  1. Has it survived 30 days of waiting?
  2. Is the emergency fund full and the future bucket paid this month?
  3. What is the full cost over five years, including running costs and what the money would otherwise have earned?
  4. How many hours of work does it cost?
  5. Am I buying the thing or the image? Would I still want it if nobody saw it?
  6. What is the resale value, and what is the cheaper version that does 90% of the job?

For a home, also: do you expect to stay seven years, and does the instalment fit inside the needs bucket with room to spare?

Committing to a partner the highest-leverage decision in the manual
  • Character. Are they honest, kind to people who can do nothing for them, and steady when things go wrong?
  • Direction. Do your pictures of an ordinary Tuesday ten years out fit together: city, work, family, money, faith?
  • Conflict. When you disagree, do you both repair it within a day? How you fight predicts more than how you get along.
  • Growth. Are you each better for the other's presence, by your friends' account and not only your own?
  • Families. Have the hard conversations about expectations, money and where you will live been had out loud?
  • Time. Have you seen each other through a bad stretch, not only good ones?

Write what you want and what you offer before you are choosing, as the People standards ask. The fuller relationship playbook comes with the People release.
