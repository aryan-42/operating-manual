01Start here

The manual holds everything. Your week runs on very little of it. This page shows which parts to use and when.

The first two weeks

  1. Day 1, 45 minutes. Do the Baseline tests you can do today and score yourself in the Scorecard. Save the snapshot.
  2. Day 2 to 7. Run the time audit and the 7-night sleep log from Baseline tests. Start Body, Stage 1, Week 1. It needs only the kit you already own.
  3. End of week 1, 60 minutes. Fill in Direction. This decides what the next ten years are for.
  4. Week 2. Set up your first 12-week sprint on the Sprint board, with Body as the focus. Put the weekly review in your calendar.
  5. Week 2, one weekend. Do the Base setup: two-step sign-in, documents scanned, room arranged. Book a first-aid class from Competence.
  6. Every quarter. Retest, rescore, archive the sprint, choose the next focus.

Three depths

  • Summary. Each domain opens with a one-page summary. That is enough to run a quarter.
  • Protocol. The staged plan underneath tells you what to do each week.
  • Playbooks. The collapsed rows marked with a plus are step-by-step guides for specific situations. Open one when you need it. "Open all playbooks" at the top of the page expands every one.

Two rules that protect the system

One focus per quarter. Every other domain runs at its Floor. Overhauling nine areas at once is the most reliable way to quit by week three.

Reading is not doing. Adding content to this manual, rebuilding it as an app, or studying a domain you are not working on all feel like progress. Only ticked standards and retested numbers count.

Everything you type or tick is saved automatically, and each standard records the date you first met it. Use the backup at the bottom of Rhythm once a year. All nine domains are now at full depth. Competence and Base are built to run in the background: one skill a quarter and one admin hour a month. Use the search box in the index to find anything by keyword.

02The standard

What "top 1% man" means here, stated so it can be measured.

You said you do not want the watered-down version. Agreed. Here is the version that is both ambitious and mathematically real.

The arithmetic of being rare

Being the best in the world at one thing is mostly unavailable. Being very good across every domain at once is rare and available. If four qualities were unrelated and you reached the top 30% in each, you would be in roughly the top 1% for the combination (0.3 × 0.3 × 0.3 × 0.3 = 0.8%). Top 25% in three is 1.6%.

Real traits are correlated, so the true figure is less extreme. The principle holds. Most people who are strong in one domain have let another collapse: the high earner who is unfit, the athlete who is broke, the achiever who is alone. The top 1% man is the one with no collapsed domain and one or two spikes.

The definition this manual uses

  • No domain below Strong. All nine domains at the Strong tier or better.
  • Two spikes at Elite. For you the natural spikes are Craft and one of Body or Presence.
  • Held for years. A standard you hold for one quarter is a stunt. The target is to still be there at 34.

03Principles

The psychology the system runs on, with an honest rating of how strong the evidence is.

Self-improvement content repeats findings that have not held up. Each principle below carries a rating: solid well replicated or consensus, useful real but smaller than advertised, contested do not build on it.

1. Confidence is a record, not a feeling solid

In Bandura's work on self-efficacy, the strongest source of belief in your ability is having actually done the hard thing before. Watching others and being encouraged help less.17 So you do not work on confidence directly. You stack small completed challenges and the belief follows. Every domain here starts with a Floor you can hit this month for that reason.

2. Pursue goals you endorse solid

Self-determination theory identifies three needs behind lasting motivation: autonomy (you chose it), competence (you are getting better), relatedness (you are connected to people while doing it). Motivation and wellbeing rise when they are met and fall when they are blocked.16 A goal you adopted to impress people fails the first test. The Direction section exists to filter those out.

3. Write if-then plans useful

"If it is 6:30 on Monday, Wednesday or Friday, then I go to the gym before anything else." A 2006 meta-analysis of 94 tests found a medium-to-large effect on goal attainment (d = 0.65).9 A much larger 2024 synthesis that corrected for publication bias put the effect far lower, between 0.15 and 0.35.10 Use it, because it costs nothing. Do not expect it to carry the load.

4. Imagine the obstacle, not just the win useful

WOOP: Wish, Outcome, Obstacle, Plan. Pairing a vivid goal with the specific inner obstacle and an if-then plan shows a small-to-medium effect across 21 studies (g = 0.34), and the authors note the true figure may be smaller.11 Pure positive visualisation is not part of this system.

5. Habits take months, and one miss is harmless solid

The study behind the "66 days" figure followed 96 volunteers for 12 weeks. The median time to automaticity was 66 days, with a range from 18 to 254, and missing a single day made no material difference.8 Only about half the participants fitted the habit curve at all. Plan on two to eight months per habit, and treat the first miss as nothing and the second in a row as an alarm.

6. Design the environment. Do not rely on willpower contested

The idea that willpower is a tank that drains has not survived testing. A preregistered project across 36 labs and 3,531 people found an effect near zero (d = 0.06), with the data four times more likely under no effect.12 Stop asking whether you have enough discipline today. Remove the decision: gym bag packed the night before, phone charging outside the bedroom, investments debited on salary day.

7. In knowledge work, hours are not the edge solid

Deliberate practice explains about a quarter of performance differences in games, a fifth in music and under 1% in professions.13 Practice still matters. In a career, the differentiators are which problems you work on, who gives you feedback and which opportunities you are near. The Craft section is built around that.

8. Learn by testing yourself, spaced out solid

A review of ten study techniques rated two as high utility: practice testing and distributed practice. Rereading and highlighting, the most common methods, rated low.14 For anything you want to keep: close the book, write what you remember, repeat after days and then weeks.

9. Skip the mindset and grit products contested

Growth-mindset programmes show an average effect on achievement close to zero (d = 0.05 across 63 studies), though a competing analysis finds real benefits for at-risk students.15 Grit turns out to be nearly the same thing as conscientiousness and only moderately related to performance.18 You do not need a special belief. You need the plain version of conscientiousness: do what you said, on schedule, checked.

10. Three thinking tools worth owning

  • The control split (Stoic). Sort every worry into "mine to act on" and "not mine". Act on the first list. Drop the second.
  • The thought record (CBT). Situation, automatic thought, evidence for, evidence against, balanced thought. Five lines on paper when your mind is running a story.
  • Values over moods (ACT). Ask "what would the person I am building do in the next ten minutes?" and do that with the mood still present.

The filter for anything new

Before adopting any technique, ask three questions. Was it tested on more than a few dozen people? Did anyone outside the original team repeat it? Would it change what I do this week? If you cannot answer yes to all three, park it in the research inbox in the repository.

05How change works

The mechanics of changing behaviour over years, so that the dip in week four does not surprise you.

Lead measures and lag measures

A lag measure is the result you want: waist size, a promotion, net worth. It moves slowly and you cannot act on it directly. A lead measure is the action that produces it: training sessions done, deep-work hours, rupees invested. You can hit a lead measure this week.

Every domain in this manual lists both. Plan and review on lead measures. Check lag measures monthly or quarterly. Judging yourself weekly on a lag measure is the fastest way to lose heart, because it barely moves in a week.

The shape of a new habit

PeriodWhat it feels likeWhat to do
Week 1 to 2Novelty carries you. It feels easy and you want to add more.Do not add. Do less than you could and never miss.
Week 3 to 7Novelty is gone and the habit is not yet automatic. This is where most plans end.Shrink the action if needed. Protect the cue and the time slot above everything.
Week 8 to 12It starts to happen without debate on most days.Now raise the difficulty, a little.
Month 4 onwardMissing it feels wrong.Free up attention for the next habit.

The timings follow the habit research: early repetitions add the most automaticity, the median plateau is around day 66, and complex habits such as exercise take longer.8 The week labels are a guide, not a finding.

Why plans fail

  • Too many changes at once. Each new behaviour competes for the same attention.
  • Outcome goals with no action attached. "Get fit" has no Tuesday.
  • No fixed cue. "When I have time" never arrives.
  • All or nothing. One miss is read as failure and the whole plan is dropped.
  • Unchanged surroundings. The same room, phone and schedule produce the same behaviour.
  • Borrowed goals. A goal you do not own gets abandoned the first time it costs something.

The four moves this manual uses

  1. One focus. A single domain per 12-week sprint, with two or three lead measures.
  2. Fixed cue. Every action has a day, a time and a place, written as an if-then plan.
  3. A floor. A version so small it survives your worst day: the minimum viable day in Rhythm.
  4. Evidence of progress. Ticked standards with dates, retested baselines, quarterly snapshots. Belief in yourself is built from that record.17

A review pooling 92 meta-analyses and more than 1.1 million people found conscientiousness to be the strongest non-cognitive predictor of performance at work. 11Assets and constraints

A plan that ignores your real starting position is a wish. State both sides plainly, once, and update them yearly.

What you start with

  • An uncommon combination. An engineering degree, an MBA in HR and the ability to build software and AI automation. Few people hold all three.
  • A job in hand. You begin your career with an offer accepted, which removes the pressure most of your cohort is under and frees the coming months for foundation work.
  • Time. At 24, ten years of compounding still leaves you at 34.
  • Proof that you ship. You already build and finish projects. That habit transfers.

How to think about constraints

A constraint is a fact that narrows your options: a credential, a location, money, an obligation, your health. It is different from an excuse, which is a constraint you have not tested. For each one, write the workaround. Most early-career constraints stop mattering once you have two or three results with numbers attached.

The lifting and running numbers are common coaching benchmarks for a healthy man in his twenties. They are judgement, not research findings. Adjust for your frame and any injuries. Your starting numbers go in Baseline tests.

What the evidence says

  • Weekly dose. WHO guidance for adults is 150 to 300 minutes of moderate aerobic activity or 75 to 150 vigorous, plus muscle strengthening for all major groups on 2 or more days.2
  • Light weights work if the sets are hard. A meta-analysis of 21 studies found similar muscle growth from light and heavy loads when sets were taken to failure. Heavy loads were better for maximal strength.22 Your home kit is enough for the first six to nine months.
  • Volume. Growth rises with weekly sets per muscle. Ten or more beats fewer than five, and 12 to 20 hard sets a week is a reasonable range once trained.4
  • Cardio fitness and lifespan. In 122,007 adults given treadmill tests, the fittest group had about a fifth of the death risk of the least fit, with no upper limit to the benefit. Observational, in clinic patients.3
  • Push-ups as a marker. Among 1,104 male firefighters followed for 10 years, those who could do more than 40 push-ups had far fewer cardiovascular events than those who managed fewer than 10. The authors caution it may not generalise beyond active middle-aged men, and it marks fitness rather than causing it.25
  • Protein. Across 49 trials, extra protein improved strength and muscle gain from lifting, with no further benefit above roughly 1.6 g per kg per day, and source did not matter.1
  • Sleep amount. The consensus is 7 or more hours a night on a regular basis.5
  • Sleep regularity. In 60,977 adults wearing activity trackers, more regular sleep and wake times went with 20 to 48% lower risk of death, and regularity predicted mortality better than duration did. Observational, in older UK adults.23
  • Caffeine. In a small lab study, 400 mg taken even 6 hours before bed cut sleep by more than an hour, and participants did not notice.24
  • Alcohol. For men aged 15 to 39, the intake that minimises health risk is about a tenth of a standard drink a day. At your age there is no health case for drinking.26
  • Waist. NICE advises keeping your waist under half your height. A ratio of 0.4 to 0.49 indicates no increased risk.6
  • Mood. A network meta-analysis of 218 trials found walking or jogging, yoga and strength training produced moderate reductions in depression, though the authors rate confidence in the evidence as low.28

How to progress

  • Rest 60 to 90 seconds between sets. Lower every rep under control.
  • Start at the bottom of the rep range. Add a rep or two each session.
  • When every set reaches the top of the range, make the exercise harder next time: a heavier dumbbell, a 3-second lowering phase, a pause at the bottom, an added band, or a one-leg or one-arm version.

  • Write every session in the training log: exercise, load, reps.

What the evidence says

  • Learning. Of ten study techniques reviewed, self-testing and spacing rated highest. Rereading and highlighting rated low.14 Matching study to a "learning style" has no adequate evidence behind it.30
  • Meditation. A review of 47 trials found mindfulness programmes gave small to moderate improvements in anxiety, depression and pain, with little or no evidence for attention, sleep or positive mood, and no advantage over other active treatments such as exercise.27 Worth ten minutes a day. Not a superpower.
  • Exercise. Walking or jogging, yoga and strength training show moderate effects on depression across 218 trials, with low confidence in the evidence.28 Your Body sessions are also mental-health sessions.
  • Willpower. The depletion effect is close to zero in large replications.12 Arrange the room. Do not rely on resolve.
  • Confidence. The strongest source of belief in your ability is having done the hard thing before.17 That is the reasoning behind the pressure drill.

The deep-work session a script for 60 to 90 minutes
  1. Before. Write the single task and what "done" looks like on paper. Phone in another room. Close mail and chat. Water on the desk.
  2. First 5 minutes. Start with the smallest concrete step. Resistance peaks at the start and fades once you are moving.
  3. During. When a stray thought or errand appears, write it on the paper and return. Do not act on it.
  4. If stuck for 10 minutes. Write down exactly what you do not understand. That usually unsticks it. If not, switch to the next sub-task, not to your phone.
  5. After. One line in the weekly notes: what you did, and where to start tomorrow. Then a real break away from screens.

Count only minutes spent on the task. A block with two phone checks is not a deep-work block.

Staying level under pressure four tools, in the order to reach for them
  1. Slow the breath. Breathe out for longer than you breathe in, for two minutes. It is the fastest lever you have on a racing body.
  2. Name it. "This is anxiety about the presentation." Naming the state puts a little distance between you and it.
  3. Split control. Two columns: mine to act on, not mine. Write one action for the first column. Leave the second.
  4. Act on values. Ask what the man you are building would do in the next ten minutes, and do it with the feeling still present. Waiting to feel ready is the trap.

The pressure drill. Calm is trained by exposure, in steps. List ten situations that make your pulse rise, from mild to severe: asking a question in a large meeting, cold-messaging someone senior, disagreeing with a manager, speaking without slides. Do one a week, starting from the mildest. Log it in the Courage log in Character.

These four tools are standard practice in cognitive and acceptance-based therapy. They are listed here as judgement, without specific trial citations.

Stillness practice ten minutes, no app required
  1. Sit upright, same place and time each day. A timer for ten minutes.
  2. Attend to the breath at the nostrils or belly. Count each out-breath from one to ten, then start again.
  3. When you notice you have drifted, and you will within seconds, return to one. That return is the repetition. It is the whole exercise.
  4. Do not judge the session. A distracted ten minutes still counts.

The alternative. Ten minutes of longhand writing: what is on my mind, what am I avoiding, what matters today. Some people get more from this. Pick one and run it for eight weeks before deciding.

Phone and screens a one-time setup that removes most of the fight
  • Remove social and video apps from the home screen. Log out after each use.
  • Turn off every notification except calls and messages from people.
  • Set app limits on the phone for the two apps that take the most time. Have someone else set the passcode.
  • Greyscale display after 9 p.m.
  • A cheap alarm clock, so the phone has no reason to be near the bed.
  • One fixed window a day for feeds and video, after the work is done.
  • Check the weekly screen report in the weekly review. It is your lead measure.

For compulsive use of any kind, including pornography and gambling apps, the same approach applies: remove access and cues first, and talk to a professional if it does not shift. The vices playbook in Character has the full method.

Decisions for anything costly or hard to reverse
  1. State it. The decision, the options including "do nothing", and the deadline.
  2. Base rate. How does this usually turn out for people in my position? Start from that, then adjust.
  3. Pre-mortem. Imagine it is a year later and it failed. Write why. Fix what you can now.
  4. Three horizons. How will I feel about this in ten days, ten months, ten years?
  5. Ask one person who has done it and has no stake in your choice.
  6. Decide, then record. In the decision journal: what I chose, what I expect, how confident I am as a percentage.
  7. Review at six months. Was the reasoning sound, separate from the result? This is how judgement improves.

Reversible decisions deserve minutes. Only irreversible ones deserve this process.

See a doctor, psychologist or psychiatrist if any of these last two weeks or more: low or flat mood most days, loss of interest in things you normally enjoy, constant worry you cannot switch off, sleep or appetite badly off, panic attacks, or using alcohol or anything else to get through the day.

  • Trading derivatives loses money for almost everyone. SEBI's own study found 93% of more than one crore individual futures and options traders lost money between FY22 and FY24, about ₹2 lakh each on average.33
  • Money and happiness. For most people happiness keeps rising with income. It stops rising only for the least happy group.20 Beyond basic security, relationships move wellbeing far more than wealth does.19

Tax basics what to understand, without the section numbers
  • India has two tax regimes: one with lower rates and few deductions, one with higher rates and more deductions. Run your own numbers through the government calculator each year and pick the cheaper one.
  • Your employer deducts tax every month based on what you declare. Declare accurately in April and again when asked for proofs.
  • Read your salary slip until you can explain every line: basic, allowances, provident fund, professional tax, tax deducted.
  • File your own return on the government portal at least once. Match it against the annual tax statement and your Form 16.
  • Capital gains on funds and shares are taxed when you sell, at rates that depend on the asset and holding period. Buying and holding defers this.
  • Never choose an investment only for a tax saving. A bad product with a deduction is still a bad product.
Credit and debt use credit, never carry it
  • A credit score runs from 300 to 900. Above 750 is generally treated as good by lenders.
  • It is built by paying on time, using a small part of your limit, and keeping old accounts open.
  • Pay the full statement amount, automatically. The minimum-due option is the most expensive loan you can take.
  • No "buy now, pay later", personal loans or instalment plans for things that lose value.
  • An education loan or a sensible home loan can be reasonable debt. Clear the costliest loan first.
  • Do not lend, or sign as guarantor, an amount you cannot afford to lose. If you lend to family or friends, treat it as a gift in your own accounts.
Big purchases phone, vehicle, home
  • The 30-day rule. For anything over an amount you set, wait 30 days. Most wants do not survive it.
  • Vehicle. A common heuristic: at least 20% down, a loan of four years or less, and total running cost under a tenth of income. Prefer no loan at all in your twenties.
  • Home. Renting is not waste when you may change city. Compare the full cost of owning (interest, maintenance, taxes, the return your down payment would have earned) against rent. Buy when you expect to stay seven years or more, and the instalment fits inside your needs bucket.
  • Status goods. Watches, phones and cars are signals. Buy them from the wants bucket after the future bucket is paid, or not at all.

What the evidence says

  • First impressions are instant. In five experiments, judgements of competence, trustworthiness and likeability made after seeing a face for a tenth of a second closely matched judgements made with no time limit. More time raised confidence, not accuracy.35 People decide fast and then look for confirmation, so the first seconds are worth preparing. The lead author also notes that these impressions are revised as people get to know you.
  • Posture tricks do not work. Power posing failed to change hormones or behaviour in replication.29
  • Composure is trained by doing. Belief in your ability comes mainly from having done the thing before.17 Speaking repetitions are the method. There is no shortcut around them.

Playbooks

Speaking clearly structure, pace and fillers
  • Answer first. Conclusion in the first sentence, then two or three reasons, then stop. "Yes, we should, for three reasons."
  • One idea per sentence. Short sentences are easier to say well and easier to hear.
  • Pause instead of filler. When you feel "um" coming, close your mouth for a beat. Silence reads as control.
  • Slow the first sentence. Nerves speed you up. Deliver the opening line deliberately and the rest follows.
  • Land the end of sentences. Do not trail off or let a statement rise like a question.
  • Numbers and examples. One specific number or story persuades more than three adjectives.

The weekly tape. Two minutes, unscripted, on any topic. Watch once with sound, once without. Note one thing to fix. Record the filler count in Baseline tests each quarter.

Presenting a ten-minute talk that works
  1. One sentence first. Write the single thing the audience should remember. If you cannot, you are not ready.
  2. Structure. Problem, what you propose, evidence, what you need from them. Three points at most.
  3. Open with the point, not with thanks, an agenda or an apology.
  4. Slides support you. Few words, one idea each. Never read them out.
  5. Rehearse aloud three times, standing, with a timer. Once in front of a person.
  6. Prepare the five hardest questions and your answers.
  7. In the room. Arrive early, test the screen, stand still when making a point, look at one person per sentence.
  8. When you do not know, say so and say when you will. It builds more credibility than a guess.
  9. End on the ask. Then stop.
Writing the one-page memo
  • Subject line or title that states the decision needed.
  • First paragraph: the recommendation and the single most important reason.
  • Then the evidence, in order of importance. Numbers with sources.
  • Then risks and what you would do about each.
  • Last line: what you need, from whom, by when.
  • Cut every sentence that would not be missed. Read it aloud before sending.

Clear writing is clear thinking made visible. It is also the fastest way to be noticed by people senior to you who never see you speak.

Listening and conversation where warmth comes from
  • Three questions before one statement. In any new conversation, ask three real questions before offering an opinion.
  • Follow the last answer. Your next question should come from what they just said, not from your list.
  • Summarise back. "So the real problem is the handover, not the tool?" People feel understood when they hear their point returned accurately.
  • Names. Say it back when you hear it. Write it down afterwards with one detail.
  • Do not top their story. Let it be theirs.
  • Leave well. End conversations a little early, with something specific: "I will send you that article."

What the evidence says

  • It decides the long run. Harvard's study of adult life has followed people since 1938. Its director's central finding is that the quality of close relationships shapes both happiness and physical health across a lifetime.19 Pooling 148 studies and 308,849 people, those with stronger social relationships had 50% greater odds of survival over the follow-up period.21
  • Isolation is a health risk. A later pooled analysis of 70 studies put the added likelihood of death at 26% for loneliness, 29% for social isolation and 32% for living alone, after adjusting for other factors.38 These are observational figures. They show a link, not a guaranteed cause.
  • Friendship is counted in hours. In two studies of people who had recently moved, it took about 50 hours together to go from acquaintance to casual friend, about 90 to become friends and more than 200 to become close friends. Hours spent only working side by side counted for less.37
  • People want to hear from you. Across a series of preregistered experiments with more than 5,900 participants, people consistently underestimated how much a message out of the blue was appreciated, and the more unexpected it was, the more it was valued.42
  • Questions build liking. In three studies of live conversations, people who asked more questions, and especially follow-up questions, were liked more by the person they were talking to.41
  • Good news is a test. People who felt their partner responded with real interest when they shared something good reported better relationships than those who got a flat or deflating response.43
  • In a partnership, the dynamic outweighs the profile. Across 43 datasets and 11,196 couples, what best predicted relationship quality was how each person experienced the relationship itself: a partner seen as committed, feeling appreciated, sexual satisfaction, a partner seen as satisfied, and low conflict. Individual traits of either person added little once those were known.39
  • Nobody can predict your relationship from a formula. The well-known claims of forecasting divorce with around 90% accuracy came from equations fitted to the same couples they described. Tested on fresh data, such equations lose much of their accuracy.40
  • Loose ties open doors. In an experiment across 20 million LinkedIn members, moderately weak ties produced more job moves than strong ones.36

Playbooks

The inner five and the contact log the whole system in one page
  1. Name five. The people whose absence would change your life. Family counts. A partner counts. Five is a working number, not a rule.
  2. Give each a rhythm. Weekly for those nearby, fortnightly for those far away. Put it in the calendar as a repeating event.
  3. Keep one log. A single note with a line per person: last real contact, what is going on in their life, what you said you would do.
  4. Read it in the weekly review. Anyone untouched for a month gets a message that day.
  5. Add an outer ring. Fifteen to twenty people you want to keep: old friends, cousins, former colleagues, mentors. Each gets a message every two or three months.

This feels mechanical for about three weeks. After that it is simply how you stay close to people while your schedule fills.

Making friends in a new city where 200 hours actually come from
  1. Choose repeating settings. A sports group, a class, a quiz league, a volunteer shift, a running club. Same people, same time, every week. One-off events almost never produce friends.
  2. Go six times before judging. The first three visits are awkward for everyone.
  3. Move it outside the setting. After a few weeks, invite one or two people to something else: food after the session, a match, a short trip. A change of context is the step from acquaintance to friend.
  4. Be the one who organises. Most people are waiting to be asked. A standing plan, such as the same dinner on the first Saturday, does the inviting for you.
  5. Count hours, not meetings. A full day out is worth six coffees.
  6. Expect most to fade. You need two or three to stick. That is a normal yield.

Colleagues can become friends, but hours at the desk count for less than hours chosen freely.37 Eat lunch with them, and see them at least once outside work.

Conversation four habits that make people glad they talked to you
  1. Ask the second question. The follow-up shows you heard the first answer. "How did that go?" then "What made it hard?"41
  2. Meet good news with interest. When someone tells you something went well, stop what you are doing, ask for the details and let them enjoy telling it. A distracted "nice" is remembered.43
  3. Remember one thing. After a conversation, add one line to the log: the interview next week, the parent in hospital, the name of the child. Ask about it next time.
  4. Say the appreciative thing out loud. If you think it, say it, with the specific reason.

The listening drill is in Presence. With strangers, the reliable openers are the setting you share and a real question about what they are working on.

  3. Be useful first. Send the article, make the introduction, fix the spreadsheet. Ask for nothing in the same message.
  4. Keep loose ties warm. Batchmates, former interns, quiz-circuit friends. A short message twice a year is enough. These are the ties jobs travel along.36

Choosing a partner what to look at, and what to stop looking at

The largest study of its kind found that who each person is on paper tells you little about how good the relationship will be. How the two of you treat each other tells you most of it.39 So judge the relationship you are actually in, over time, and give less weight to the checklist.

  1. Write it down first. What you want and what you offer, before you are choosing anyone. Keep it short: character, direction, how conflict is handled.
  2. Watch five things. Do they act committed? Do you feel appreciated, and do they? Is conflict rare and repaired quickly? Are you both satisfied, including physically? These are the predictors that held across the research.
  3. See them across situations. Under stress, with their family, with people who serve them, when they lose, when plans collapse. A bad stretch tells you more than a good holiday.
  4. Have the plain conversations early. City, careers, money and debts, children, parents and who lives with whom, faith and food. Out loud, not assumed.
  5. Ask two people who know you. "Am I better or worse around this person?" Listen to the answer.
  6. Take the time. A decision that will run for fifty years can wait until you have seen a full year.

If your family is involved in the choice, the same tests apply. Ask for enough time and enough private conversation to run them. The commitment framework is in Decisions.

Steps 3 to 6 are judgement. No one can forecast a specific couple's future with the confidence popular books claim.40

Being a good partner maintenance, done weekly
  • Appreciation, daily and specific. "Thank you for handling the landlord" beats "thanks for everything".
  • Turn toward small bids. When they point something out or start a story, give it your attention. Most of a relationship is made of these moments.
  • A weekly check-in. Twenty minutes: what went well between us, what is coming up, anything unsaid. Small things raised early do not become large ones.
  • Carry your share of the home without being asked or thanked. The Competence section exists partly for this.
  • Keep your own life. Friends, training and work that are yours. Two whole people make a better pair.
  • Protect time. One evening a week that is not moved for work.

These are judgement and common practice in couples work, consistent with the predictors above.

Conflict and repair with anyone who matters to you
  1. Cool down first. If your pulse is up, say "I want to get this right, give me twenty minutes" and go and calm down. Venting does not reduce anger. Slowing down does.45
  2. One issue. The thing that happened today. No history, no "you always".
  3. Describe, feel, ask. "When the plan changed without a message, I felt dropped. Next time, tell me before."
  4. Say their side back until they agree you have it. Then make your point.
  5. Apologise properly. What you did, the effect it had, what you will do differently. No "if" and no "but".
  6. Close it. Agree the next step and do not reopen it the following week.

Repair within a day when you can. An unrepaired small thing is how distance starts.

Boundaries and endings saying no, and leaving well
  • Say no early and plainly. "I can't take that on" needs no long reason. A late no costs more than an early one.
  • Separate the person from the pattern. Name the behaviour, once and clearly, before you pull back.
  • Reduce before you cut. Most draining relationships need less time, not a dramatic ending.
  • End a romantic relationship in person, clearly and kindly, without leaving a door half open to be kind. Then give it distance.
  • Leave at once and get help if anyone threatens you, controls your money or movement, or makes you afraid. That is not a rough patch.

  1. Message two people from the outer ring today.
  2. Book one call with an inner-five person this week.
  3. Add one repeating setting, and give it six visits.
  4. Keep training and sleep steady. Both make contact easier.

If it lasts more than a few weeks alongside low mood, use the "When to get help" playbook in Mind.

What the evidence says

  • Self-control compounds for decades. In a study that followed 1,000 children from birth to age 32, childhood self-control predicted adult health, finances and criminal convictions, separately from intelligence and family background. Those whose self-control improved as they grew up did better than their early scores predicted.44
  • It is built from the outside in. Willpower as a tank that drains did not hold up across 36 labs.12 The practical reading: arrange your life so that the temptation is not in the room.
  • Venting anger does not work. Across 154 studies and 10,189 people, activities that calm the body, such as slow breathing, relaxation and meditation, reduced anger and aggression. Activities that raise arousal, including hitting a bag and going for a run, had no reliable effect.45
  • Purpose is linked to a longer life. A pooled analysis of prospective studies found that people with a strong sense of purpose had a lower risk of death and of cardiovascular events. This is observational, so it shows a link, not a cause.46
  • Giving lifts the giver, a little. A registered replication found that people who spent money on others ended up happier than those who spent it on themselves. Treat the effect as small.47
  • Gratitude exercises are modest. A meta-analysis found small effects on symptoms of depression and anxiety. Worth doing, not a cure.48
  • Two substances with clear numbers. For men aged 15 to 39, the alcohol intake that minimises health risk is close to zero.26 Smokers lose at least a decade of life on average, and stopping before 40 avoids about 90% of the extra risk.49

Playbooks

Writing the code ten lines you will not cross
  1. Start from costs. List five times you acted in a way you respect and five you do not. The code is what separates the two lists.
  2. Write lines you can fail. "I am a good person" cannot be broken. "I do not take credit for work I did not do" can.
  3. Test each line with a price. Would you hold it if it cost you a month's pay, a promotion or a friendship? If not, rewrite it until it is true.
  4. Ten lines or fewer. You must be able to recall it in a hard moment.
  5. Review it quarterly and after any decision you are not proud of. Change a line only when you have outgrown it, never to excuse something.

Examples of the form, not a template: "I say the true thing, kindly, the first time I am asked." "I do not make a promise I have not written down." "I treat the person who can do nothing for me as I treat the person who can."

Honesty in practice four levels, in order
  1. No false statements. Including the easy ones: "on my way", "I never got the mail", the rounded-up number, the extra year on a skill.
  2. No engineered false impressions. A technically true sentence built to mislead is a lie with better grammar.
  3. Say the hard true thing, kindly and early. The honest feedback, the "I disagree", the "I cannot deliver that by Friday".
  4. Be honest with yourself. The scorecard, the money numbers and the vice audit are only useful if they are true.

Privacy is not lying. "I would rather not say" is honest. So is declining to share an opinion that would only hurt.

On a CV and in an interview, every number should survive a phone call to the person who was there.

The promise ledger why your word starts to mean something
  1. For 30 days, write down every commitment as you make it, however small: the callback, the file, the "let's catch up".
  2. Mark each one kept, renegotiated before the deadline, or late or broken.
  3. Count the third column at the end. That is your baseline in Baseline tests.
  4. Then make fewer promises. Replace "I'll try" and "soon" with a date or a no.
  5. When you will miss one, say so before the deadline and offer a new one.

Most people find they break small promises daily without noticing. Fixing that is the fastest change in how others rate your reliability.

Anger and ego what to do in the first ten minutes
  1. Lower the heat. Slow breathing with a long out-breath, cold water on the face, a slow walk. Punching something or going for a hard run to "burn it off" does not reduce anger.45
  2. Say nothing and send nothing until your pulse is normal. Write the reply if you must, and leave it unsent overnight.
  3. Name what was threatened. It is nearly always status, control or fairness. Naming it shrinks it.
  4. Ask what outcome you want a week from now, and act for that.
  5. Go back and repair anything you said before you caught it.

Vices and compulsions an audit, then bright lines

  5. Tell one person. Secrecy is what keeps a compulsion alive.

Meaning and inner life the part that holds the rest up
  • Know what the work is for. The mission line in Direction. Reread it when a week feels pointless.
  • Keep a practice. Prayer, sitting, a text you return to, time in nature, a journal. Use the tradition you were raised in or one you choose. What matters is that it is regular and yours.

  • Think about the end, once a year. At the annual review, write what you would want said about you by the people who knew you best. Then look at last month's calendar.
  • Do something with no payoff. Rest, play and adventure are in Base. A capable, joyless man has failed the standard.
When you break your own code you will
  1. Admit it to the person affected, the same day, without a defence.
  2. Make it right in the currency it cost them: money, time, reputation.
  3. Find the setup. What made it easy? Tiredness, a deadline, drink, wanting to be liked. Change that.
  4. Keep the line. One breach is a reason to hold the code more carefully, never a reason to delete the line.
  5. Then stop punishing yourself. "I did a bad thing" leads to repair. "I am bad" leads to hiding.

What the evidence says

  • The road is the largest risk at your age. India recorded more than 4.8 lakh road accidents in 2023. Among those killed, 54,568 were riders or passengers on two-wheelers who were not wearing a helmet.53 A review of the trials and studies found that helmets cut the risk of death by about 42% and of head injury by about 69%.54

  • Drowning is common and preventable. The WHO counted about 300,000 drowning deaths worldwide in 2021.52 Being able to swim and to stay afloat is a safety skill before it is a sport.

Cooking ten meals that fit your diet

Running a home the weekly minimum
  • Daily, ten minutes: bed made, dishes done, surfaces clear, tomorrow's clothes out.

Swimming and water from non-swimmer to safe
  1. Take lessons from a coach, two or three times a week for eight to twelve weeks. Adults learn quickly with instruction and slowly without it.
  2. Learn in this order: breathing out under water, floating on your back, treading water, then a stroke.
  3. The safety test comes before the distance test: fall in, surface, float, and get to the side.
  4. Then build to 200 metres at any pace.

  5. Devices: screen lock, automatic updates, disk encryption, remote-wipe switched on.
  6. Backups: automatic, to the cloud and to a drive. Test by restoring a file.
  7. Payments. You enter a UPI PIN only to send money, never to receive it. Never share a one-time code or install a screen-sharing app because a caller asks. Keep a low daily limit on the account linked to your phone.
  8. Links and calls. Do not sign in through a link in a message. Open the app or type the address. Banks, police and courier companies do not ask for codes, and nobody is "arrested" over a video call.
  9. What you post. No documents, boarding passes, home address or live location. Assume an employer will read all of it.

If money has been taken, act within the hour: call your bank to block the account or card, then report it on the national cybercrime helpline, 1930, or at cybercrime.gov.in. Speed is what gets money frozen.

24Seasons

Some weeks are not normal weeks. Each kind has a plan, so that a hard stretch pauses the system without ending it.

The rule for every season

  1. Name it. "This is an exam fortnight." A named season is a decision. An unnamed one is a slide.
  2. Give it an end date. Write the day normal service resumes.
  3. Hold the floor of the floor: sleep, the three non-negotiables from Rhythm, and one message to someone in the inner five.
  4. Drop the rest on purpose, without guilt. The sprint board pauses. It does not reset.
  5. Restart on the named day, at half volume for the first week.
Exam weeks one to three weeks
  • Hold: wake time, 7 hours of sleep, three 20-minute training sessions a week, real meals.
  • Drop: reading for pleasure, the side project, new habits, hosting.
  • Study as Mind teaches: self-testing and spaced review, in single-task blocks, phone in another room.
  • Do not trade sleep for hours. A tired hour of revision is worth less than half a rested one.
  • Return: the day after the last paper is off. Normal week from the following Monday.
Joining a job the first four weeks
  • Hold: sleep, three training sessions, the call home, the weekly review.
  • Drop: every non-work project for a month. The job is the focus.
  • Fix the anchors in week one: wake time, commute, when and where you train, what you eat at lunch.
  • Run the first-90-days playbook in Craft and the relocation checklist in Base.
  • Expect to be tired. New places cost energy. It passes by week six.
Moving city about a month
  • Hold: sleep, a short daily walk or session, the weekly review, the inner-five calls.
  • Drop: progress targets in Body. Aim only to not lose the habit.
  • First week: find the place you will train and go once, even briefly. The habit survives a move if it restarts within seven days.
  • Run the relocation checklist in Base and the move plan in People.
Travel a few days to two weeks
  • Hold: the minimum viable day, protein at each meal, wake time within an hour.
  • Pack: resistance band, running shoes, the book.
  • Train: the 15-minute session from "Busy days and travel" in Body, every second day.
  • On holiday, hold nothing except sleep. A holiday is rest, and rest is part of the system.
  • Return: first session within 48 hours of landing.
Illness or injury until you are well, plus a week
  • Hold: sleep, fluids, food. Nothing else.
  • Do not train with a fever, chest symptoms or anything below the neck. Walking is fine when you feel like it.
  • See a doctor if a fever lasts beyond three days, if breathing is difficult, or for any injury that stops normal movement.
  • Return at half: half the weight or half the distance in the first week back, then build over two weeks.
  • For an injury, train what is not injured. The injury rules are in Body.

General guidance, not medical advice.

Festivals, weddings and visits home days to two weeks
  • Hold: a short morning session or walk, and your bedtime on most nights.
  • Enjoy the food. Eat what is served and stop when full. One week does not undo a quarter.
  • Decide your line on alcohol before you arrive, not at the table.
  • Be fully there. This time is People at its most valuable. Put the phone away.
  • Return: the first Monday back, no compensation, no punishment sessions.
A crunch at work two to six weeks
  • Hold: sleep at 7 hours, two training sessions a week, one evening off.
  • Drop: the sprint, social plans except the inner five, anything optional.
  • Set the end date with your manager, out loud. A crunch without one is the new normal.
  • If it passes eight weeks, it is no longer a season. Read the burnout entry in the Risk register.
Heartbreak weeks to months
  • Hold: sleep, training, meals, work at an acceptable level. Structure carries you when motivation cannot.
  • Lean on people. Tell two friends. See someone most days for the first fortnight.
  • Remove the cues: mute or unfollow, archive the chats, put the objects away. This is kindness to yourself, not drama.
  • No big decisions for a month: no job changes, no moves, no rebound commitments.
  • No alcohol to cope.
  • Write it out once, on paper: what was good, what was not, what you will do differently.
  • If sleep, appetite or work stay badly off after a month, use "When to get help" in Mind.

Judgement and common practice.

A family emergency as long as it takes
  • Go. Tell work plainly and early what you need.
  • Hold: sleep where you can, food, a daily walk.
  • Take a role. Paperwork, money, transport, sitting with someone. Families in a crisis need someone handling logistics.
  • Use the reserve. This is what the emergency fund and the parents' medical line in Money are for.
  • Keep a notebook: doctors' names, what was said, what was paid, what is due.

25Accountability

A record you can see and a person who sees it. Both raise the odds that a plan gets done, and both take minutes a week.

What the evidence says

Across 138 experiments with 19,951 people, prompting people to monitor their progress improved goal attainment, with an average effect of d = 0.40. The effect was larger when progress was physically recorded and when it was reported to someone or made public.50 That is the case for the sprint board, the training log and the weekly message below.

Setting it up

  1. Choose one person. Someone reliable, working on a goal of their own, who will notice a missed week and say so. A batchmate, a sibling, a training partner. It should not be someone you manage or report to.
  2. Agree the format. One message on the same day each week: your three lead measures as numbers, one line on what got in the way, one line on next week. They send theirs.
  3. Reply in one line. Acknowledge, and ask one question when a number drops two weeks running.
  4. A call every month, twenty minutes, to look at the trend.
  5. Share the quarterly snapshot with them, including the lowest score.
  6. Review the arrangement each quarter. If either of you has missed three messages, end it kindly and find another.

Some work is better in company: a training partner who expects you at 7 a.m., a study group, a class you have paid for. Use these freely. They are accountability you do not have to manage.

26When it breaks

It will. The plan for failure is part of the system.

If the cause is a stretch with a name and an end date, such as exams, a move or an illness, use Seasons. For the larger shocks, see the Risk register.

You missed a day

Nothing happened. In the habit research a single missed day had no material effect.8 Do the minimum viable day tomorrow. The rule is never two in a row.

You missed two weeks

  1. Do not restart on Monday. Restart within the hour, with the smallest version.
  2. Run only the three non-negotiables for seven days.
  3. In the weekly review, write what changed in your environment. Travel, a new job and a breakup are the usual causes. Fix the environment and leave your character out of it.

You have plateaued

  • Body: check sleep and food before changing the programme.
  • Craft: you have probably outgrown your feedback. Find a harder problem or a tougher reviewer.
  • Everything: a flat quarter after two strong ones is consolidation. Hold the Floor and wait.

You are burning out

Signs: sleep worsening, dreading things you used to like, getting ill often, snapping at people. Drop to Floor in every domain for two weeks, protect sleep, see your inner five. If it persists, see a doctor.

The standard has turned on you

"Best who can ever be" is a direction. Used as a verdict on your worth, it produces the Wayne failure: a capable, joyless man. Two checks each quarter. Did I enjoy anything that had no payoff? Would the people in my inner five say I have been good to be around? If either answer is no, that is the constraint to fix next, ahead of any score.

You compared yourself to someone

Compare with your own snapshot from a year ago. That is the only comparison with the same starting conditions.

34Glossary

Terms the manual uses, in one place.

The system

Domain
One of the nine areas the manual tracks: Body, Mind, Craft, Money, Presence, People, Character, Competence and Base.
Standard
A specific, checkable statement in a domain. You tick it when you have met its check, and the date is recorded.
Floor, Strong, Elite
The three tiers of standards. Floor is the minimum that keeps a domain from collapsing. Strong is clearly above average and sustainable. Elite is rare and takes years.
Focus
The one domain you are pushing this quarter. Every other domain runs at its Floor.
Sprint
Twelve weeks on one focus, tracked on the Sprint board with three lead measures.
Lead measure
Something you do each week and fully control, such as training sessions completed.
Lag measure
A result that follows months later, such as a faster 5 km.
Hit rate
The share of weekly lead-measure targets you met in a sprint.
Snapshot
Your scores for all domains on one date, saved from the Scorecard.
Constraint
Your lowest-scoring domain. It usually chooses the next focus.
Baseline
A measured starting number, recorded once and never changed.
Non-negotiables
Three daily actions small enough to do on your worst day.
Minimum viable day
The 15-minute version of the day for travel, illness or chaos.
Season
A named stretch with an end date in which most of the system is paused on purpose.
Tripwire
A rule set in advance that forces a decision when a number crosses a line.
Gate
A test a project must pass before it gets more of your time or money.
Playbook
A step-by-step guide for one situation, shown as a collapsed row in a domain.
Phase and exit test
One of the five stages of the Roadmap, and the conditions for leaving it.

Practices

Deep work
Single-task concentration on something that matters, with the phone out of the room.
Recall note
What you can write about a chapter from memory after closing the book.
Thought record
A five-line written exercise from cognitive behavioural therapy for testing a worrying thought against evidence.
Pressure drill and courage rep
One deliberately uncomfortable action a week, taken from a ladder of situations ordered by difficulty.
If-then plan
"If situation X happens, then I will do Y." Also called an implementation intention.
WOOP
Wish, outcome, obstacle, plan. Imagining the obstacle before making the if-then plan.
Prep file
Five lines written before any meeting that matters: what they want, what you want, the hard questions, your answers, and what you do if the answer is no.
Wins log
A running list of results you delivered, each with a number.
Inner five
The five people who matter most to you, each given scheduled time.
Outer ring
Fifteen to twenty further people you keep in touch with every few months.
Mentor and sponsor
A mentor advises you. A sponsor speaks for you when you are not in the room.
Weak tie
An acquaintance. New jobs and ideas tend to arrive through these.
The code
Ten or fewer written lines you will not cross.
Promise ledger
A written list of commitments, each marked kept, renegotiated or broken.
Bright line
A rule with no exceptions, used where moderation keeps failing.
Savings rate
Money saved or invested, divided by money received.
Emergency fund
Months of expenses held in cash for shocks.
Admin hour
One hour a month for bills, renewals, backups and repairs.

Reading the evidence

Solid, useful, contested
The manual's ratings. Solid is well replicated or expert consensus. Useful is real and smaller than advertised. Contested means do not build on it.
Judgement
Advice from practice and reasoning that no study in this manual supports. It is always labelled.
Meta-analysis
A study that pools the results of many studies on the same question.
Replication
A repeat of a study by other researchers. A preregistered one fixes its method in advance.
Observational
A study that watches what people do without assigning them to anything. It can show a link, and cannot by itself show a cause.
Effect size, d or g
How large a difference is, in standard-deviation units. Around 0.2 is small, 0.5 medium and 0.8 large, by convention.
Odds ratio
How much higher the odds of an outcome are in one group than another. 1.0 means no difference.
