00Character

What you do when it costs you and nobody is checking. Everything else in this manual amplifies whatever is here.

One-page summary

The standard
Your word holds, you say true things, your temper and appetites do not make your decisions, and part of your time and money goes to someone else.
Three actions
1. Write the code, ten lines or fewer. 2. Keep a promise ledger for 30 days. 3. One courage rep a week, logged.
Watch one number
Promises late or broken in the last 30 days.
This quarter
Stage 1 takes one evening to set up and five minutes in each weekly review.

Standards

Floor month 1 to 3

Strong year 1 to 3

Elite year 4 onward

Character has few numbers to measure. These standards are judgement. The checks are there so that a tick means something happened, and is more than a feeling about yourself.

What the evidence says

  • Self-control compounds for decades. In a study that followed 1,000 children from birth to age 32, childhood self-control predicted adult health, finances and criminal convictions, separately from intelligence and family background. Those whose self-control improved as they grew up did better than their early scores predicted.44
  • It is built from the outside in. Willpower as a tank that drains did not hold up across 36 labs.12 The practical reading: arrange your life so that the temptation is not in the room.
  • Venting anger does not work. Across 154 studies and 10,189 people, activities that calm the body, such as slow breathing, relaxation and meditation, reduced anger and aggression. Activities that raise arousal, including hitting a bag and going for a run, had no reliable effect.45
  • Purpose is linked to a longer life. A pooled analysis of prospective studies found that people with a strong sense of purpose had a lower risk of death and of cardiovascular events. This is observational, so it shows a link, not a cause.46
  • Giving lifts the giver, a little. A registered replication found that people who spent money on others ended up happier than those who spent it on themselves. Treat the effect as small.47
  • Gratitude exercises are modest. A meta-analysis found small effects on symptoms of depression and anxiety. Worth doing, not a cure.48
  • Two substances with clear numbers. For men aged 15 to 39, the alcohol intake that minimises health risk is close to zero.26 Smokers lose at least a decade of life on average, and stopping before 40 avoids about 90% of the extra risk.49

Protocol by stage

Stage 1Weeks 1 to 12
  • Write the code. Read it in each weekly review for the first month.
  • Keep the promise ledger for 30 days.
  • Do the vice audit once, honestly, and act on the top line.
  • One courage rep a week, logged.
Stage 2First year at work
  • Use the anger steps until they are automatic. Nothing sent while angry.
  • Set the giving: a fixed monthly amount or a fixed monthly slot.
  • Ask the yearly question: "What is the thing about me that nobody tells me?"
  • Decide your lines on expenses, credit and data before the first time they are tested.
Stage 3Year 3 onward
  • As you gain rank, run the power checks every quarter.
  • Mentor. Teach what you were taught.
  • Reread the mission in Direction against your calendar. Change one of them.

Playbooks

Writing the code ten lines you will not cross
  1. Start from costs. List five times you acted in a way you respect and five you do not. The code is what separates the two lists.
  2. Write lines you can fail. "I am a good person" cannot be broken. "I do not take credit for work I did not do" can.
  3. Test each line with a price. Would you hold it if it cost you a month's pay, a promotion or a friendship? If not, rewrite it until it is true.
  4. Ten lines or fewer. You must be able to recall it in a hard moment.
  5. Review it quarterly and after any decision you are not proud of. Change a line only when you have outgrown it, never to excuse something.

Examples of the form, not a template: "I say the true thing, kindly, the first time I am asked." "I do not make a promise I have not written down." "I treat the person who can do nothing for me as I treat the person who can."

Honesty in practice four levels, in order
  1. No false statements. Including the easy ones: "on my way", "I never got the mail", the rounded-up number, the extra year on a skill.
  2. No engineered false impressions. A technically true sentence built to mislead is a lie with better grammar.
  3. Say the hard true thing, kindly and early. The honest feedback, the "I disagree", the "I cannot deliver that by Friday".
  4. Be honest with yourself. The scorecard, the money numbers and the vice audit are only useful if they are true.

Privacy is not lying. "I would rather not say" is honest. So is declining to share an opinion that would only hurt.

On a CV and in an interview, every number should survive a phone call to the person who was there.

The promise ledger why your word starts to mean something
  1. For 30 days, write down every commitment as you make it, however small: the callback, the file, the "let's catch up".
  2. Mark each one kept, renegotiated before the deadline, or late or broken.
  3. Count the third column at the end. That is your baseline in Baseline tests.
  4. Then make fewer promises. Replace "I'll try" and "soon" with a date or a no.
  5. When you will miss one, say so before the deadline and offer a new one.

Most people find they break small promises daily without noticing. Fixing that is the fastest change in how others rate your reliability.

The courage ladder one rep a week

Courage is trained in steps, the same way as the pressure drill in Mind. They share one log.

RungExamples
LowAsk the question you think is stupid. Admit you do not know. Return a wrong order politely.
MiddleGive honest feedback to a peer. Say no to a senior's unreasonable ask. Apologise first. State an unpopular view in a meeting.
HighReport something wrong. Walk away from a deal or a job on principle. Tell someone close a truth they do not want.

Pick one a week from the lowest rung you still avoid. Write the date, what you did and what happened. The feared result rarely arrives, and the log is the proof.

Anger and ego what to do in the first ten minutes
  1. Lower the heat. Slow breathing with a long out-breath, cold water on the face, a slow walk. Punching something or going for a hard run to "burn it off" does not reduce anger.45
  2. Say nothing and send nothing until your pulse is normal. Write the reply if you must, and leave it unsent overnight.
  3. Name what was threatened. It is nearly always status, control or fairness. Naming it shrinks it.
  4. Ask what outcome you want a week from now, and act for that.
  5. Go back and repair anything you said before you caught it.

Ego checks, quarterly. When did I last say "I was wrong" without adding a reason? Do I need to win small arguments? Do I mention my own results before I am asked? Can I be the least capable person in a room and stay curious?

Steps 2 to 5 and the ego checks are judgement.

Vices and compulsions an audit, then bright lines

The audit. For each of these, write how often, what it costs in money, hours, sleep and self-respect, and what sets it off: alcohol, nicotine, other drugs, gambling and trading for the thrill, pornography, endless video and feeds, gaming, junk food, impulse buying.

  1. Sort into two lists. Things you can take or leave, and things you repeatedly do more of than you meant to. Be honest about which list each belongs on.
  2. For the second list, use a bright line. "None" is easier to hold than "a little". Moderation rules fail at exactly the moment they are needed.
  3. Remove access and cues. Delete the app, block the site, do not keep it at home, change the route, leave the group chat. Design beats resolve.12
  4. Fill the slot. Decide in advance what you do at the time and in the mood where it used to happen.
  5. Tell one person. Secrecy is what keeps a compulsion alive.
  6. A slip is data. Write what set it off, close that door, continue. Do not turn one slip into a week.

If you cannot hold a line you have set three times, that is the point to see a professional. The "When to get help" playbook in Mind lists where to start.

Integrity at work decide your lines before they are tested
  • Credit. Name the people who did the work, in writing, every time.
  • Numbers. Never present a figure you would not defend line by line. If a result is weak, say so first.
  • Expenses and time. Claim what you spent and worked. Small padding is how larger habits begin.
  • Data. In HR you will hold pay, health and performance records. Open only what the task needs, and never use it to satisfy curiosity.
  • Conflicts of interest. Disclose them before anyone asks.
  • When asked to do something wrong, ask for it in writing, state your concern once to the person, then to their manager or the proper channel. Know in advance what you would resign over.
Handling power checks for when you lead

As you gain money, rank and strength, the test becomes how you treat people who can do nothing for you. That is the difference between Wayne and the men he fights.

  • Do the most junior people on my team disagree with me in meetings? If not, I have made it unsafe.
  • Do I keep one person close whose job is to tell me I am wrong?
  • Do I treat drivers, guards, cleaners and vendors as I treat directors?
  • Have I used my position for a personal favour this quarter?
  • Do I take the blame upward and pass the credit down?
  • Would I be comfortable if my decisions about people were read out to them?
Service turning 5% into a habit
  1. Pick one cause, close to something you have lived: education, a hometown, a skill you can teach.
  2. Choose time or money by what you have. As a student, time and skill. Once earning, a fixed amount on salary day, automated like an investment.
  3. Give skill where you can. An hour of building a tool for a small organisation is often worth more than the same hour of general help.
  4. Keep it regular and quiet. A fixed slot each month. No announcement.
  5. Start in your own circle. Teach a junior, help a batchmate prepare, look after a relative. That counts.
Meaning and inner life the part that holds the rest up
  • Know what the work is for. The mission line in Direction. Reread it when a week feels pointless.
  • Keep a practice. Prayer, sitting, a text you return to, time in nature, a journal. Use the tradition you were raised in or one you choose. What matters is that it is regular and yours.
  • Three lines at night, a few times a week. What went well, who helped, what I would do again. The measured benefit is small and real.48
  • Think about the end, once a year. At the annual review, write what you would want said about you by the people who knew you best. Then look at last month's calendar.
  • Do something with no payoff. Rest, play and adventure are in Base. A capable, joyless man has failed the standard.
When you break your own code you will
  1. Admit it to the person affected, the same day, without a defence.
  2. Make it right in the currency it cost them: money, time, reputation.
  3. Find the setup. What made it easy? Tiredness, a deadline, drink, wanting to be liked. Change that.
  4. Keep the line. One breach is a reason to hold the code more carefully, never a reason to delete the line.
  5. Then stop punishing yourself. "I did a bad thing" leads to repair. "I am bad" leads to hiding.

How this domain fails, and what to ignore

  • Treating character as a self-image. It is a record of what you did when it cost something.
  • Small exceptions under pressure. Every large breach was preceded by ten small ones that seemed reasonable.
  • Discipline that turns into contempt for people who have less of it.
  • Hiding a habit. What you conceal grows.
  • Ignore: "alpha" and dominance advice, cold-shower and abstinence challenges sold as character, stoicism used as a reason to feel nothing, and anyone who teaches that honesty is for people without leverage.
