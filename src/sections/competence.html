00Competence

The practical skills a capable man has. Each is learned once and kept for life, and several of them are the difference between helping and standing by.

One-page summary

The standard
You can feed yourself and others, get yourself anywhere safely, keep someone alive until help arrives, fix what breaks at home, and be at ease in a place you have never been.
How it runs
One skill per quarter, in the background. It is rarely the quarter's focus. It uses two or three hours a week from the life-admin and rest budgets.
Three actions
1. Helmet or seat belt on every trip, starting today. 2. Book a hands-on CPR and first-aid class. 3. Learn one new meal a week until you have ten.
Watch one number
Skills finished, out of the list below.

What the evidence says

  • The road is the largest risk at your age. India recorded more than 4.8 lakh road accidents in 2023. Among those killed, 54,568 were riders or passengers on two-wheelers who were not wearing a helmet.53 A review of the trials and studies found that helmets cut the risk of death by about 42% and of head injury by about 69%.54
  • CPR before the ambulance more than doubles survival. Across 30,381 witnessed cardiac arrests in Sweden, 10.5% of people who received CPR before the ambulance arrived were alive after 30 days, against 4.0% of those who did not.51 The figures are observational, and the link held after adjusting for other factors.
  • Drowning is common and preventable. The WHO counted about 300,000 drowning deaths worldwide in 2021.52 Being able to swim and to stay afloat is a safety skill before it is a sport.

Playbooks

On the road the habits that matter most
  • Helmet: a certified full-face helmet that fits snugly, strap fastened every time. An unfastened helmet comes off in a crash. Carry a second one if you give lifts.
  • Seat belt: front and back, including in cabs.
  • Phone: in a pocket or a mount, never in the hand. Set navigation before you move.
  • Speed and space: leave a gap you could stop in. Slow down at night, in rain and near junctions.
  • Never after drinking, and do not ride with anyone who has been.
  • Tired is impaired. On a long drive, stop every two hours.
  • As a pedestrian, assume you have not been seen.

The helmet figures are research.54 The rest is standard road-safety practice.

First response a reminder of the steps, not a substitute for a class

Take a hands-on course. Skills like these are learned on a manikin, not from a page. Use the list below to refresh what the class taught you.

SituationSteps
Collapsed, not responding, not breathing normallyShout for help. Call 112 or have someone call. Start chest compressions: heel of the hand on the centre of the chest, arms straight, push hard and fast, about two a second, without stopping. If a defibrillator is nearby, send someone for it and follow its spoken instructions. Continue until help takes over.
Choking, cannot speak or coughFirm blows between the shoulder blades, then abdominal thrusts, alternating, until it clears. If they collapse, start CPR.
Heavy bleedingPress hard on the wound with a cloth and do not lift it to look. Add more cloth on top. Keep pressing until help arrives.
BurnCool under running water for 20 minutes. Remove rings and watches. Cover loosely. No ice, butter or toothpaste.
Unconscious but breathingRoll them onto their side with the head tilted back, and stay with them.
Road crashMake the scene safe first. Call 112. Do not move someone who may have a neck or back injury unless they are in immediate danger.

Kit at home: sterile gauze, crepe bandage, adhesive dressings, antiseptic, medical tape, scissors, gloves, oral rehydration salts, a thermometer, and your own regular medicines.

These steps follow standard first-aid teaching and are listed as a reminder. Guidelines are revised every few years, which is one more reason to retake the class.

Cooking ten meals that fit your diet
  1. Start with five techniques, since most meals are built from them: boil and simmer, pressure-cook, sauté, shallow-fry, and temper spices.
  2. Build the ten around protein. For a diet of vegetables, fish and eggs, a workable set is: a dal, a rajma or chana, an egg curry, an omelette or bhurji, a fish curry, a pan-fried fish, a paneer or tofu dish, a mixed vegetable sabzi, a khichdi or pulao, and one breakfast you can make in ten minutes.
  3. One new meal a week. Cook it once with the recipe, once without, and write your version in the workbook.
  4. Stock the basics so a meal is always possible: rice, atta, two dals, eggs, onions, tomatoes, ginger, garlic, oil, salt, and the six spices you use most.
  5. Cook double and eat it twice.
  6. Clean as you go. A meal is finished when the kitchen is.

The protein targets and sample days are in the nutrition playbook in Body.

Running a home the weekly minimum
  • Daily, ten minutes: bed made, dishes done, surfaces clear, tomorrow's clothes out.
  • Weekly, ninety minutes: laundry washed and put away, floor and bathroom cleaned, sheets changed, groceries bought from a standing list, bin out.
  • Monthly: bills checked, the fridge and cupboards cleared, anything broken fixed or booked.
  • Skills to pick up once: iron a shirt, sew on a button, remove a stain, descale a kettle, read a meter.

Paying for help with cleaning or cooking is a fair use of money once you earn. Knowing how to do it yourself first is the point.

Swimming and water from non-swimmer to safe
  1. Take lessons from a coach, two or three times a week for eight to twelve weeks. Adults learn quickly with instruction and slowly without it.
  2. Learn in this order: breathing out under water, floating on your back, treading water, then a stroke.
  3. The safety test comes before the distance test: fall in, surface, float, and get to the side.
  4. Then build to 200 metres at any pace.

Rules that do not bend. Never swim alone or after drinking. Do not enter rivers, canals or the sea where locals do not. If someone is in trouble, reach or throw something to them before you consider going in.

Driving and vehicles licence, then real practice
  • Get the licence properly. Then log 20 hours with an experienced driver beside you before driving alone.
  • Practise the hard parts on purpose: reversing, parallel parking, hill starts, night, rain, a highway merge.
  • Know your vehicle: tyre pressure, changing a wheel, jump-starting, topping up fluids, what each warning light means.
  • Keep documents current and in one place: licence, registration, insurance, pollution certificate.
  • After any crash: stop, make it safe, help the injured, call 112, take photographs, exchange details. Do not argue fault at the roadside.
Home repairs and tools five jobs and a small kit

Kit: screwdriver set, adjustable spanner, pliers, hammer, tape measure, utility knife, tester, insulation and thread-seal tape, a drill when you have your own place, and a torch.

Jobs to learn: reset a tripped breaker and find what tripped it, replace a tap washer, hang a shelf with wall plugs, unblock a drain, fix a puncture, replace a switch or a plug with the mains off, assemble flat-pack furniture.

Know where the main water valve and the main switch are in any home you live in. Leave gas and anything inside the distribution board to a professional.

Travelling alone one week, planned by you
  1. Pick a place a little outside your comfort. A different state, a different language.
  2. Book the first and last nights only. Leave the middle open.
  3. Split your money and documents: one card and some cash on you, one card and copies in the bag, digital copies in your phone and your mail.
  4. Share the outline with one person at home, and check in daily.
  5. Pack for one bag you can carry for an hour.
  6. Talk to people. Eat where it is busy. Walk more than you ride.
  7. Write a page a day. What you noticed, what went wrong, what you handled.
Hosting a dinner for six
  • Invite a week ahead, with a time. Ask about food restrictions.
  • Cook what you have made before. One main, two sides, something sweet you bought.
  • Do everything possible before people arrive, so you are in the room and out of the kitchen.
  • Introduce people to each other with one thing they have in common.
  • Keep glasses and plates full, and let the conversation run.
  • Message a thank-you the next day.

Hosting monthly is a People standard. Table manners are in the etiquette playbook in Presence.

Self-defence what it is really for
  1. Awareness. Head up, phone away, know the exits. Most trouble is avoided by noticing it early.
  2. Leave. Pride is the usual reason fights happen. Walking away is the win.
  3. De-escalate. Hands open, voice low, give the other person a way out.
  4. Hand over property. A phone and a wallet are replaceable.
  5. Train anyway. Six months of boxing, judo, wrestling or jiu-jitsu gives you fitness, calm under physical pressure, and an accurate sense of what a fight costs. That accuracy is what keeps trained people out of fights.

This is judgement. Force is a last resort for getting away from immediate danger.

How this domain fails, and what to ignore

  • Watching videos about a skill in place of doing it. A skill is done when your hands have done it twice.
  • Starting four at once. One per quarter finishes. Four do not.
  • Skipping the dull, high-value ones (the helmet strap, the CPR class) for the impressive ones.
  • Ignore: survivalist shopping lists, self-defence "moves" learned from clips, and the idea that paying someone means you never need to know how.
