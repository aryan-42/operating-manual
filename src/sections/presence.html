00Presence

How you look, sound and carry yourself. Competence people can see within seconds, backed by substance that survives the next hour.

One-page summary

The standard
You look put together, speak clearly and briefly, listen well, and stay composed when challenged. People leave a conversation with you feeling heard and sure of what you said.
Three actions
1. Fix fit and grooming once, then keep them on a schedule. 2. Answer first, then explain. 3. One speaking repetition a week, recorded or logged.

What the evidence says

  • First impressions are instant. In five experiments, judgements of competence, trustworthiness and likeability made after seeing a face for a tenth of a second closely matched judgements made with no time limit. More time raised confidence, not accuracy.35 People decide fast and then look for confirmation, so the first seconds are worth preparing. The lead author also notes that these impressions are revised as people get to know you.
  • Posture tricks do not work. Power posing failed to change hormones or behaviour in replication.29
  • Composure is trained by doing. Belief in your ability comes mainly from having done the thing before.17 Speaking repetitions are the method. There is no shortcut around them.

Most specific advice on voice, clothing and body language rests on convention and experience more than on strong studies. The playbooks below are judgement unless a source is given.

Protocol by stage

Stage 1Months 1 to 3
  • One-time fixes: get clothes altered, set the grooming schedule, replace worn shoes.
  • Record a 2-minute talk every week. Count fillers. Fix one thing.
  • Practise answer-first in every reply in class, in mail and in messages.
Stage 2Months 4 to 18
  • Take every chance to present. Join a speaking club or start one. Reach 25 logged repetitions.
  • Write one page a week: a memo, a project write-up or a public post.
  • Ask two people you trust what your presence is like when you are stressed.
Stage 3Year 2 onward
  • Larger rooms, tougher audiences, unscripted questions.
  • Negotiations with real stakes.
  • Refine a style that is yours: fewer, better things.

Playbooks

Speaking clearly structure, pace and fillers
  • Answer first. Conclusion in the first sentence, then two or three reasons, then stop. "Yes, we should, for three reasons."
  • One idea per sentence. Short sentences are easier to say well and easier to hear.
  • Pause instead of filler. When you feel "um" coming, close your mouth for a beat. Silence reads as control.
  • Slow the first sentence. Nerves speed you up. Deliver the opening line deliberately and the rest follows.
  • Land the end of sentences. Do not trail off or let a statement rise like a question.
  • Numbers and examples. One specific number or story persuades more than three adjectives.

The weekly tape. Two minutes, unscripted, on any topic. Watch once with sound, once without. Note one thing to fix. Record the filler count in Baseline tests each quarter.

Presenting a ten-minute talk that works
  1. One sentence first. Write the single thing the audience should remember. If you cannot, you are not ready.
  2. Structure. Problem, what you propose, evidence, what you need from them. Three points at most.
  3. Open with the point, not with thanks, an agenda or an apology.
  4. Slides support you. Few words, one idea each. Never read them out.
  5. Rehearse aloud three times, standing, with a timer. Once in front of a person.
  6. Prepare the five hardest questions and your answers.
  7. In the room. Arrive early, test the screen, stand still when making a point, look at one person per sentence.
  8. When you do not know, say so and say when you will. It builds more credibility than a guess.
  9. End on the ask. Then stop.
Writing the one-page memo
  • Subject line or title that states the decision needed.
  • First paragraph: the recommendation and the single most important reason.
  • Then the evidence, in order of importance. Numbers with sources.
  • Then risks and what you would do about each.
  • Last line: what you need, from whom, by when.
  • Cut every sentence that would not be missed. Read it aloud before sending.

Clear writing is clear thinking made visible. It is also the fastest way to be noticed by people senior to you who never see you speak.

Listening and conversation where warmth comes from
  • Three questions before one statement. In any new conversation, ask three real questions before offering an opinion.
  • Follow the last answer. Your next question should come from what they just said, not from your list.
  • Summarise back. "So the real problem is the handover, not the tool?" People feel understood when they hear their point returned accurately.
  • Names. Say it back when you hear it. Write it down afterwards with one detail.
  • Do not top their story. Let it be theirs.
  • Leave well. End conversations a little early, with something specific: "I will send you that article."

Wardrobe for an Indian climate and office, at three budgets

Principles. Fit matters more than brand. Shoulders must fit off the rack, and a tailor fixes the rest for a small cost. Plain, dark and well kept beats loud. Breathable cotton and linen blends for the heat. Buy less and replace what is worn.

Checks. Shirt collar closes with one finger of room. Sleeves end at the wrist bone. Trousers touch the shoe with one small break. Nothing pulls across the chest or seat. Iron or steam everything.

Body language and room behaviour what is worth doing
  • Stand and sit tall, shoulders relaxed. Take up your space without spreading.
  • Keep your hands visible and still. Gesture when making a point, then rest.
  • Look at people when they speak and when you finish a sentence.
  • Move slowly. Hurry signals low status in any room.
  • Phone face down or away, always.
  • In meetings, speak in the first ten minutes, even to ask one question. It gets harder the longer you wait.
  • On video: camera at eye level, light in front of you, look at the lens when making your main point.
Etiquette dining, meetings and travel
  • Introductions. Stand, look at the person, say your full name clearly. Introduce the junior person to the senior one.
  • Dining. Wait for the host to start. Order something easy to eat. Match the pace of the table. Treat serving staff well. People watch this more than anything else.
  • Who pays. Whoever invited. Offer once, sincerely, and accept the answer.
  • Mail and messages. Reply within a day, even if only to say when you will answer properly. Check names and spellings before sending.
  • Meetings. Agenda in advance if you called it. End on time. Send the decisions and owners afterwards.
  • Thank-yous. Specific and within 24 hours.
  • Travel. Carry-on packed light, documents ready, early to the gate. Calm travellers are noticed.
Staying composed when challenged the Specter moment
  1. Pause. One breath out before answering. It looks like thought, because it is.
  2. Restate the challenge fairly. "You are saying the numbers do not hold for smaller teams." This buys time and shows you are not threatened.
  3. Concede what is true. "That is right for teams under ten."
  4. Then make your point, once, with the evidence.
  5. Lower your voice as theirs rises.
  6. If it is an insult, not a challenge, name the topic and return to it. "Let us stay with the proposal." Do not trade.

How this domain fails, and what to ignore

  • Copying the swagger without the preparation. On screen, the one-liners work because the character already won the case in the library the night before.
  • Performing confidence in place of listening. Talking more is not presence.
  • Spending on brands before fit.
  • Ignore: power poses, "alpha" body-language rules, the claim that 93% of communication is non-verbal, pickup-style tricks, and any script designed to pressure or mislead. Skill and honesty are the whole of this section.
