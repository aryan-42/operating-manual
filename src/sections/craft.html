What the evidence says

  • Hours are not the edge. Accumulated practice explains less than 1% of performance differences in professions, against 18 to 26% in sports, music and games.13 What separates people in knowledge work is problem selection, feedback and proximity to opportunity.
  • Reliability is. Conscientiousness is the strongest non-cognitive predictor of job performance across more than a century of studies. The effect is weaker in highly complex jobs.31 Early in a career, being the person whose work never needs checking is a real advantage.
  • Acquaintances find you jobs. An experiment across 20 million LinkedIn members found that moderately weak ties created more job moves than close ties, most strongly in digital industries.36 A wide, lightly maintained network matters more than a few close contacts for mobility.
  • Asking pays. In a survey of 149 new hires in the United States, those who negotiated raised their starting pay by about $5,000 on average, mainly through competing and collaborating strategies. Small sample, one country.34
  • Preparation over posture. Power posing did not change behaviour in replication.29 The prep file is the real version.

Protocol by stage

Before joiningNow to day one
  • Close the two weakest boxes in your skill stack. Use real HR data problems, not tutorials.
  • Build one portfolio piece that shows the intersection: an HR process automated end to end, written up with before and after.
  • Learn to read a P&L and a balance sheet. Read your employer's last annual report.
  • Find three people already in the company or function and ask each one question.
CredibilityYear 0 to 2
  • Be excellent at the job you were hired for. Nothing else counts until this is true.
  • Find the most painful manual process near you and automate it. Measure hours or money saved.
  • Choose managers over brand names. A strong boss in year one is worth more than a title.
  • One win per quarter into the log. One feedback conversation per month.
AdjacencyYear 2 to 5
  • Move one step toward revenue: HR tech product, people analytics, consulting, total rewards or a business role. One step at a time is realistic. A direct leap rarely is.
  • Publish what you build. A portfolio does the selling when you are not in the room.
  • Keep a small HR and AI tool alive on the side with a fixed weekly time budget and written stop criteria.
  • Start managing: an intern, a project team, a vendor.
OwnershipYear 5 to 10
  • Take P&L responsibility, or found the company if the side tool has paying users.
  • Build a team. Your ceiling becomes the quality of people who choose to work with you.
  • Add income that does not depend on your salary.

The prep file the whole Specter effect in ten minutes

Before any meeting that matters, write five lines.

  1. What they want from this meeting.
  2. What I want, as one sentence.
  3. The three objections or hard questions I will get.
  4. My answer to each, with the number or example that supports it.
  5. What I do if the answer is no.

Add the names and roles of everyone attending and one thing each of them cares about. Almost nobody does this, which is why it reads as confidence.

Visibility and sponsors making sure good work is known
  • The Friday note. Three lines to your manager each week: done, next, blocked. It becomes your appraisal evidence.
  • One-page write-ups. Every finished project gets a page: problem, what you did, result in numbers.
  • Demo, do not describe. Show the thing working to the people who feel the problem.
  • Mentor and sponsor are different. A mentor advises you. A sponsor puts their name on you. You earn a sponsor by making a senior person's goal succeed.
  • The advice loop. Ask one specific question, act on the answer, report back within a month. That loop is what turns a senior acquaintance into a mentor.
  • Keep weak ties alive. A short message twice a year to former classmates, colleagues and alumni: one useful link or a real congratulation.
Negotiating pay offers, raises and role changes
  1. Know the range. Three sources: people in the role, recruiters, published data. Write the low, middle and high.
  2. Know your alternative. The best thing you will do if this fails. A second offer is the strongest. Build it before you need it.
  3. List your evidence. Wins with numbers from the log.
  4. Let them name a number first where you can. If asked, give a range whose bottom is a figure you would accept gladly.
  5. Ask plainly, then stop talking. "Based on the scope and what I have delivered, I am looking for X." Silence after the ask is part of it.
  6. Trade, do not concede. If base pay cannot move, ask about joining bonus, review date, role scope, learning budget or location.
  7. Stay collaborative. You will work with these people. Aim for an agreement both sides can defend.
  8. Get it in writing.

A campus offer with fixed terms for a whole cohort leaves little to negotiate at entry. The first real negotiation is usually the first appraisal or the first external offer. Prepare the evidence from day one.

Office politics, done honestly how decisions really get made
  • Find out who decides, who influences, and who can block. They are often three different people.
  • Before a proposal, talk to each person it affects, one at a time. A meeting should confirm a decision, not surprise anyone.
  • Give credit in public, by name. It costs nothing and people remember.
  • Disagree in private first. In the room, disagree once, clearly, then commit to the decision.
  • Never put in writing what you would not say to the person's face.
  • Do not join factions. Be useful to both.
  • When someone takes credit for your work, correct the record calmly the same day, with the document. Then move on.
Leading people the basics for your first project team
  • Set the outcome, not the steps. Say what done looks like and by when. Let them choose how.
  • One-to-ones. Thirty minutes a week with each person. Their agenda first.
  • Feedback. Specific, soon, private for criticism. "When you did X, the effect was Y. Next time, try Z."
  • Delegate what you are good at. That is the only way your capacity grows.
  • Protect the team. Take blame upward and pass praise downward.
  • Hire slowly. A work sample beats an interview impression.

How this domain fails, and what to ignore

  • Planning the third job while underperforming in the first.
  • Learning five tools shallowly. Depth in one visible thing beats a long skills list.
  • Letting the side project eat the main job. Credibility comes first.
  • Doing good work silently. If nobody with influence knows, it did not happen for career purposes.
  • Changing jobs only for pay. Change for scope, manager and learning, and pay follows.
  • Ignore: hustle-culture hour counts, certificates collected without a project, personal-brand advice that puts posting ahead of doing, and anyone who says HR cannot lead to business roles.
